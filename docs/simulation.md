# Simulation

How a frame turns into particle movement. Code: `src/simulation.ts`, `src/elements.ts`, `src/element-table.ts`, `src/cell-fields.ts`, `src/cell-field-buffers.ts`, `src/shaders/cell.wgsl.ts`, `src/shaders/elements.wgsl.ts`, `src/shaders/ca.wgsl.ts`, `src/shaders/brush.wgsl.ts`, `src/movement.ts`, `src/physics.ts`, `src/flyers.ts`, `src/shaders/flyers.wgsl.ts`, `src/passes.ts`, `src/heat.ts`, `src/shaders/heat.wgsl.ts`, `src/air.ts`, `src/air-grid.ts`, `src/shaders/air.wgsl.ts`, `src/toolbar.ts`; the drawing is in `docs/rendering.md`.

## Frame pipeline

Each animation frame runs `stepsPerFrame` supersteps (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 24 or 32; the speed labelled "1x" is 4), then the render passes. All of it is recorded into one command encoder and sent with one `submit()` (`main.ts`). A frame is:

1. **Brush**: while a mouse button is down, the brush pass, one thread per cell (each thread touches only its own cell). Then the air's passes that run once a frame: the mask pass, the heat exchange, and the Wind tool's pass when the pointer moved while it is selected (see Air).
2. **Supersteps**: `stepsPerFrame` of them, each two CA dispatches with Margolus offset 0, then 1, then the flyer passes (see Flyers), then the air steps (see Air). The dispatches update the cell buffers in place. The brush, every CA dispatch and the flyer passes share one compute pass, handed out by `PassRecorder` (`src/passes.ts`); the bench gives each group of dispatches a timed pass of its own instead.
3. **Overlay and render**: the flyers are scattered into the overlay buffer, then the render passes draw the cell buffers and the overlay with the brush ring (see `docs/rendering.md`).

Every CA dispatch needs its own `{ offset, seed }`. They live in 256-byte slots (`minUniformBufferOffsetAlignment`) of one uniform buffer sized for the 64 dispatches of a 32-superstep frame; one `writeBuffer` fills the used slots and each dispatch selects its slot with a dynamic offset. The grid size is a shader constant.

At most every 2 seconds, on frames where no mouse button is down, a separate compute pass counts non-empty, non-wall cells with an atomic counter, adds the flyers in the air, and reads the total back asynchronously for the toolbar. It has its own submit.

## Cell format

A cell is one 32-bit word in each of the field buffers listed in `CELL_FIELDS` (`src/cell-fields.ts`), one storage buffer per field (`src/cell-field-buffers.ts`):

- `kind`: bits 0–7 `type`, the element ID (a row of the element table below); bits 8–15 `variant`, random 0–255 assigned at paint time (the display reads it as the cell's grain, `docs/rendering.md`); bits 16–23 `ctype`, the element a molten cell sets into (0 for every other cell, see Phase changes); bits 24–31 reserved.
- `life`: for an element with a lifetime (fire), the time left. For a liquid (water, oil), the sideways direction the cell last moved in (0 none, 1 left, 2 right).

- `velocity`: the cell's velocity in cells per dispatch, x in the low 16 bits and y in the high 16 bits, each a binary16 float (`pack2x16float`). y = 0 is the bottom row, so a falling cell has a negative y.
- `remainder`: the part of the cell's movement it has not spent yet, packed the same way. A cell moves one cell inside its block when the remainder on an axis reaches 1, so a speed of 1/4 cell per dispatch is a move every four dispatches. Both words are 0 for anything that does not fall, and for a cell at rest.
- `temp`: the stored temperature in °C, an `f32` (the TypeScript side reads the words through `tempView`). It means nothing for a cell that holds no heat, empty space and walls: `makeCell`, the eraser and Clear leave it 0. A painted cell starts at its element's `temperature`, and so does every cell of a scene built by `src/scenes.ts` or loaded without a `temp` field (`Simulation.load`; a scene may set other temperatures, as `heatBars` does). Conduction (phase 7) and the phase changes (phase 8) change it for the cells that hold heat; a cell carries it unchanged wherever it moves or flies. For a cell inside a phase change it can lie past the element's threshold (see Phase changes).

The shaders see them as a `Cell` struct (`loadCell`, `storeCell`, `storeChanged`, `cellType`, `cellVariant`, `cellLife`, `cellVelocity`, `cellRemainder`, `cellStoredTemp`, `makeCell`, `withType`, `withLife`, `withMotion`, `withTemp`), generated from the field list with the bindings in `@group(1)`, binding = field index. A new field is one entry in `CELL_FIELDS`; `makeCell` starts it at 0, and the `with…` builders copy a cell and change only the named part. `storeChanged` compares an `f32` field by its bits, so a moved cell keeps its exact word (as floats, −0 equals 0).

Cells travel as whole structs: swapping two cells swaps every field.

Intuition for the layout: each field is its own array, like the columns of a table, so a pass can later skip the columns it does not need (today every pass binds all of them), and the device's buffer size limit applies to one column at a time. The CA updates the arrays in place instead of writing a second copy: a thread loads its block's four cells, runs the rules, and writes back only the fields that differ from what it loaded. This is safe because blocks do not overlap, so a rule must read and write only the four cells of its own block.

The states: *empty* is air; a *solid* never moves and nothing passes through it; *powders* and *liquids* fall, and liquids also spread sideways; *gases* rise or sink by their density against the air's; *plasma* (fire) stays where it is, but a gas rises through it. A row also holds a label, a color and a `temperature` (°C, default 20: what a painted or loaded cell starts at). Its physical properties are in SI-based units, from data with the source in the row's comment: a `conductivity` (W/m·K, default 0), a `heatCapacity` (MJ/m³·K per cell, above 0, default 1), optional `below` and `above` transitions (a threshold in °C, the element it becomes and its latent heat in MJ/m³; see Heat; an `above` also takes a `burst`, see Bursts), a liquid's `viscosity` (Pa·s) and optional `densityKnots`, a gas's `densityTemp`, a powder's `repose` (degrees) and a crushable solid's `toughness` (MPa·√m) with its `pressureAbove` (the element it crushes into; see Pressure transitions). It may also have an optional `rule`, an optional `graphics` function and an optional toolbar order. A powder or liquid row has a `gravity` (cells per dispatch squared, default 1/32) and a `drag` (the share of its speed relative to the air it loses per dispatch, in the grid and in flight, default 1/64; see Air moves matter); other states have no gravity.

An element *holds heat* when its conductivity is above 0 or it has a transition (`elementHoldsHeat`, the table's `holdsHeat` word): its cells' `temp` words mean something. Empty space and the wall hold none. WALL is the engine's invincible wall and the world's edge, not a real material: it never conducts, so heat never leaves the world; the real building materials are metal and stone. SMOKE is a placeholder for the smoke of every fire, and its heat values are too (backlog I16). Every element models a real material with physical properties; one that does not says so in its row.

The CA, the brush and the display read the table as a uniform array of 256 rows of 144 bytes (a uniform array's stride is a multiple of 16) (`@group(2)`; `ElementTable` fills it, `PROP_FIELDS` lists a row's words and generates both the WGSL struct and the packer). A row is 36 words: the color, density, state, flammable flag, life start and rule, gravity, drag, the derived `inert` and `holdsHeat` flags, temperature, conductivity and heat capacity, then the transition words: `belowTemp`, `belowWidth`, `aboveTemp`, `aboveWidth`, `transitions` (the element ID each transition leads to, in 8 bits each: below in bits 0–7, above in 8–15, the pressure transition in 16–23), `aboveBurst` and `pressureAbove` (an `f32`, `NO_PRESSURE` = 3·10³⁸ when the element has none: a finite value that no pressure reaches, since infinity may not compare the same on every GPU; derived from the row's toughness), then the density by temperature (`densityMode`, four temperatures and four densities), `spread` (a liquid's chance to move sideways per dispatch, 1 for everything else, derived from its viscosity), `slide` (a powder's sideways speed off a step, 0 for everything else, derived from its angle of repose), `molten` (1 for lava and molten metal, whose cells carry a ctype), `shockLimit` (the temperature difference that cracks a solid, 0 for none) and one word of padding. In the `transitions` word, bits 16–23 (the pressure target) also hold the crack target of thermal shock, and bits 24–31 the element an `above` into a molten element sets into. A uniform array indexed by the cell's type is one cached read, where a `const` array may be copied into every thread's memory. The element IDs and states are also generated as WGSL constants (`EL_SAND`, `ST_POWDER`), so no shader names a number. A test or a variant of the game gives `Simulation` another table (and extra rule sources) through its options.

Intuition: a new material only has to say what it is, powder or liquid or gas, and how heavy it is; the CA then knows it falls, piles up, sinks through lighter things and floats on heavier ones. Example: a powder row with density 1300 (mud) falls like sand, sinks through water (1000) and stays on top of sand (1600) with no other change (`tests/element-table.test.ts`).

## Margolus neighborhood

Intuition: the grid is cut into 2×2 blocks, and each block is updated on its own, so two particles can never try to move into the same cell. On the next step the block grid shifts by one cell diagonally, so particles cross block borders over time.

Example with offset 0, the block whose bottom-left cell is (4, 6) holds (4, 6), (5, 6), (4, 7), (5, 7). With offset 1 the cell (4, 6) belongs instead to the block starting at (3, 5), as its top-right cell.

One thread handles one block: it reads the four cells, runs all rules and writes back the changed fields. Block `b` has its bottom-left cell at `2·b − offset`, and there are `ceil((W + offset) / 2) × ceil((H + offset) / 2)` blocks, so at offset 1 (and at odd sizes) edge blocks stick out of the grid.

A block whose four cells are all inert (empty or solid, with no lifetime, no rule, no heat and no pressure transition: `elementInert`, the table's `inert` word) cannot change in any phase, so the thread reads only their `kind` words and stops. Most of a screen is empty space or walls, so most threads stop there. A thread keeps its block in four separate variables, not in an array: on the RTX 4080 an array indexed by a slot computed at run time sits in slow per-thread memory instead of registers, which made the CA about three times slower.

y = 0 is the bottom row; gravity points toward lower y.

A gas hot enough to rise fast (smoke painted at 150 °C, steam at 110 °C) moves one cell per dispatch, two per superstep. Falling cells start slower and speed up (see phase 1).

## Randomness

`pcg()` is the PCG-RXS-M-XS 32-bit hash. A block's random word is `pcg(bx ^ pcg(by ^ seed))`, with `bx`, `by` the block indices and `seed` a `u32` from `crypto.getRandomValues`, drawn per dispatch. Later random choices in the same block chain `pcg()` on the previous word. A choice between two options uses the top bit.

## Rules inside a block

Phases run in order on the block's four cells (BL, BR, TL, TR), each phase seeing the previous phase's result. Movement depends on the table's `state` and `density`, and for powders and liquids on the cell's velocity. With air, a block that holds a powder, liquid, gas or fire cell first samples the air once, at the block's center (see Air moves matter); a block without one skips the sample.

0. **Gases rise or sink** (`buoyancy`, see Density by temperature): a gas cell swaps with the cell above it when it is lighter than the air and that cell is empty, with the chance its density gives; a gas heavier than the air sinks into the empty cell below by the same rule; and a gas rises at once through plasma (fire) or a liquid heavier than the gas (smoke through water and oil).
1. **Falling cells move by velocity** (`velocityRule`, the bottom row first, the column order random): a powder or liquid cell adds its element's `gravity` to its vertical speed when it is in the top row or already moving (a cell at rest starts falling only once a dispatch has seen the cell below it free, or every level of a resting stack would bank one more step than the one under it and the top of a deep layer would launch), with air then loses `drag` of its speed relative to the air and is pushed by the air's pressure gradient (see Air moves matter), adds its velocity, at most 1 cell per axis, to its remainder, and when the remainder reaches 1 on an axis swaps with the cell in the way if `canDisplace` allows it: the cell is empty, or a gas, powder or liquid lighter than the mover. So sand (1600) sinks through water (1000) and oil (900), water through oil, and the displaced cell moves up. A cell never displaces one of its own density, and nothing displaces a solid or fire. A blocked axis loses its speed, except that a cell blocked by a slower cell moving the same way takes that cell's speed, so a falling column falls together. A cell faster than 1.5 cells per dispatch does not move in the grid; the flyer passes take it. Resting and sliding are part of the same phase (`velocityRule`): a powder or liquid cell resting on something that does not give way loses a quarter of its sideways speed (below 1/256 it stops), and moves to the opposite bottom corner if `canDisplace` allows it. A settled pile ends with velocity and remainder exactly 0. Two more rules sit in this phase: a powder that slides to the opposite bottom corner leaves the step with the sideways speed `slide` of its angle of repose, which carries it over the steps below until friction stops it (see Angle of repose), and a liquid slides there only with the chance of its viscosity (see Viscosity). After the falling cells, **convection** (see Density by temperature) swaps the vertical pairs of one liquid whose upper cell is denser.
2. **Gases spread**: a top-row gas cell moves into the empty neighbor in a randomly picked direction (one try); a bottom-row one does too when it cannot rise. Only a cell that did not move earlier in the dispatch moves again. With air, a further step follows the dispersion: **gases and fire drift with the air** (see Air moves matter).
3. **Liquids spread** (`spreadRow`, top row then bottom row): a liquid cell that did not move earlier in the dispatch and is not falling moves into the empty cell beside it in its row, if that is the direction it remembers (a cell without memory picks one at random) and its viscosity lets it (see Viscosity). The memory is the cell's `life` field: a cell that moved keeps going in that direction on later dispatches, which makes a stream flow instead of diffuse. A cell facing anything but empty space turns around, and one that would move turns around one time in 64 instead. Two liquids side by side never swap, so a layered body stays layered.
4. **Lifetimes**: an element with a `life` row loses its `decay` per dispatch and becomes the element named by `becomes` when it runs out (fire becomes smoke at 0).
5. **Element rules**: for each cell, a generated `switch` on its element calls that element's rule, if the row has one; the rule sees the block and may change any of its cells. Fire's rule: fire next to water becomes smoke; otherwise a burning cell is raised to at least fire's temperature (1,000 °C), so fire heats what it touches however it was lit.
6. **Ignition**: a flammable cell (oil) with a fire cell in its block becomes fire, starting at fire's `life.start`. Which cells ignite is decided right after the lifetimes (phase 4), before the rules, and applied here, so a rule never sees a cell that ignites in the same dispatch: oil that ignites beside water burns for a step before its fire goes out, while the fire that lit it is put out at once. Oil that is hot enough also becomes fire, as a transition (phase 8). With air, a cell whose element's `above` transition becomes fire (gunpowder) releases that transition's `burst` into its air cell as it ignites (see Bursts); oil's `burst` is 0, so it releases nothing.
7. **Heat conduction**: the four cells of the block exchange heat pairwise, see Heat. Before the pairs exchange, a solid with a shock limit is marked when a neighbor in its block is further from its temperature than the limit (see Thermal shock).
8. **Phase changes**: a cell whose stored temperature has run past a threshold of its element, by the whole latent width, becomes the element beyond it, see Phase changes; with air, a cell that passes an `above` threshold also releases that transition's `burst` (see Bursts). After the temperature thresholds the phase checks the pressure of the cell's air cell against the element's `pressureAbove` and, past it, turns the cell into the element beyond it (see Pressure transitions). Last, a cell marked in phase 7 cracks into its broken form (see Thermal shock). Without any transition in the table, or with only pressure transitions and no air, the phase is left out of the shader.

Fire has no movement rule; it only moves when a gas rises through it.

Intuition for the liquids: each cell looks only at its 2×2 block, so a liquid cannot feel the weight of the water above it or push water along a pipe. A pool flattens because surface cells slide off the high spots, and falls into every gap. Example: a 30×40 block of water against the left wall of a 200-wide tank spreads out to a sheet 6 to 7 cells deep in about 1,200 supersteps (`tests/simulation.test.ts`, "dam break"). Connected vessels do not level: in the `uTube` scene the filled arm stays full and the empty arm stays empty, as in The Powder Toy (`docs/decisions.md`).

## Heat

Intuition: a hot cell warms the cell next to it and cools down itself, until the two have the same temperature, and a metal passes heat on much more readily than stone. Every cell that holds heat has a temperature (`temp`); empty space and the wall hold no heat in the cell grid and stay out of the block exchange below. Empty space has the temperature of its air cell instead, and a cell that holds heat trades heat with that air (see Heat and the air), so a hot cell in open air cools down; in a box sealed by walls its heat stays inside.

Each dispatch, in phase 7 (after the rules and ignition, before the phase changes), the four cells of a block exchange heat in four pairs: the two rows, then the two columns at offset 0, the two columns, then the two rows at offset 1, so neither axis always goes first. Over a superstep every cell exchanges with each of its four neighbors, so a pair meets once per superstep (`PAIR_TIME`, 4.17 ms). The grid has a physical scale (`src/physics.ts`, `docs/decisions.md`): one cell is `CELL_SIZE` = 1.36 mm and one dispatch `DISPATCH_TIME` = 2.08 ms, the only pair for which the default gravity of 1/32 cell per dispatch² is 9.81 m/s² at 1x, so conductivities in W/m·K and capacities in MJ/m³·K are the real ones and heat moves at its real speed. For a pair (a, b) with conductivities κa, κb (W/m·K) and heat capacities Ca, Cb (MJ/m³·K):

- the pair's conductivity is the harmonic mean g = 2·κa·κb / (κa + κb), and 0 when either κ is 0 (the plain formula is 0/0 for two cells that hold no heat, which would write NaN into empty cells);
- the share s = min(1, `CONDUCTION` · g · (1/Ca + 1/Cb)) with `CONDUCTION` = `PAIR_TIME` / `CELL_SIZE`² / 10⁶ = 2.25·10⁻³ (`heatShare`, `src/heat.ts`);
- with d = Ta − Tb, the difference of the two cells' temperatures (`cellTemp`: the stored temperature clamped to the element's thresholds, see Phase changes), the stored Ta −= s·d·Cb / (Ca + Cb) and Tb += s·d·Ca / (Ca + Cb); nothing is written when g or d is 0, so a cell that holds no heat keeps its `temp` word bit for bit.

Because Ca·ΔTa + Cb·ΔTb = 0, the heat C·T is conserved up to rounding; each exchange moves both cells toward their capacity-weighted mean and never past it (the difference of their temperatures becomes d·(1 − s) with s at most 1; a cell inside a phase change is at its threshold while its stored value moves), so any conductivity and any capacities are stable. A very good conductor is capped: a row of 2,000 W/m·K shares 1 and never passes the mean. The exchange reads and writes only the block's cells, which `tests/block-locality.test.ts` checks. The capacity is per cell: a cell is one unit of matter whatever its state, so a gas row's capacity is its specific heat times its liquid's density (steam: 2.0 kJ/kg·K times water's 1,000 kg/m³ is 2.0 MJ/m³·K), and a latent heat is per kilogram times the density of the denser phase (water freezes at 334 and boils at 2,257 MJ/m³). Each row's comment names the source of its numbers.

Intuition for the speed: heat spreads like a smudge, and how fast depends on the thermal diffusivity α = κ / C (m²/s), the conductivity over the heat a volume holds per degree. Over a length L the evening-out of a bar takes a time of the order L²/α. In the table's terms a pair of cells of one material closes the share s = 2 · `PAIR_TIME` · α / `CELL_SIZE`² of its difference per superstep: for steel (κ 50, C 3.5, α 1.4·10⁻⁵ m²/s) 0.064, for basalt (2.0 and 2.4) 0.0038, for water (0.6 and 4.18) 0.00065.

Example (`tests/heat.test.ts`): a sealed bar with one half at 100 °C and the other at 0 °C relaxes as a single decaying mode, and the difference between its halves falls with the time constant τ = L² / (π² α) within 15%, for steel, stone and ice. For a bar of 50 cells (6.8 cm) that is 33 s for steel and about 9 minutes for basalt (1x is 240 supersteps a second). Single pairs of steel, stone, water and a test row of 2,000 W/m·K close exactly the share the formula gives.

### Phase changes

Intuition: ice at 0 °C does not melt the moment it gets a little heat; it sits at 0 °C and takes in a fixed amount of heat (the latent heat) first, and only then becomes water. A cell keeps that bookkeeping in its stored temperature: while it takes in its latent heat the stored temperature runs past the threshold, and the temperature it shows and conducts at stays at the threshold. Once the stored value has run past by the whole latent amount, the cell changes element.

Example: water has capacity 4.18 and boils at 100 °C with latent heat 2,257, so its latent width is 2,257 / 4.18 = 540 °C. Water stored at 400 is boiling water at 100 °C that has taken in 300·4.18 of the 2,257 it needs; stored at 640 it becomes steam. Heat that flows into it meanwhile raises the stored value and nothing else, and a neighbor sees a cell at 100 °C all along, so a boiling surface keeps drawing heat at the full rate until it has paid for the change.

The rule (`transitionCell` in `ca.wgsl.ts`, phase 8): with a threshold T, a latent heat L and a capacity C, the latent width is w = L / C. `cellTemp` is the stored temperature clamped to [below.temp, above.temp] of the element (an absent threshold is ±3e38, so the clamp needs no branch). A cell whose stored temperature s passes above.temp + w becomes the element above it, with the stored temperature T + (s − T − w) · C_old / C_new, and with the new element's life (`retype`); one under below.temp − w likewise downward. The heat C·s plus a per-element offset is the same before and after, so the change creates and destroys no energy; `latentOffsets` in `tests/metrics.ts` works the offsets out from the table, and `tests/heat.test.ts` checks the total of a box across every threshold between the paired elements over 2,000 supersteps (a relative change of 6e-8). The stored value may run well past `ABSOLUTE_ZERO` or `MAX_TEMP` while it does (steam condensing runs about 1,130 °C under 100 °C): validation accepts it, and `cellTemp`, the readout and the views stay inside the range. A new cell starts at or past the threshold on the side it came from, so it does not turn back at once; a large overshoot can carry it through the next change in the same direction on the next dispatch (ice stored at 1,000 °C becomes water, then steam).

A molten element (lava, molten metal: `molten: true`) remembers what it melted from. An `above` into a molten element writes its `setsInto` (default: its own row) as the cell's ctype, and a molten cell with ctype k sets back into k, not into its own `below`: `cellTemp` clamps it at k's `above.temp`, and phase 8 turns it into k with ctype 0 once its stored temperature runs under that by k's latent width (k's latent heat over the molten element's capacity). So melted stone sets back into stone, and melted steel into steel, though both are the one molten row. With ctype 0 (or one that names no row whose `above` becomes the molten element) the molten row's own `below` applies, as before. The molten cell moves by k's density ordering, the readout names it by what it sets into, and the display tints it by k's color. `withType` and `retype` clear the ctype; `tests/molten.test.ts` checks all of it on a table of its own.

A pair that turns into each other (water and ice, water and steam, stone and lava, metal and molten metal) shares one threshold and one latent heat, and the table is rejected otherwise (`validateElements`). A cell has to give back a whole latent heat to change back, so a surface between steam and water does not flicker: `tests/heat.test.ts` packs a box with ice over water, and one with steam over water, and no cell changes element in 500 supersteps. Oil above 250 °C becomes fire (one way, no latent heat); the fire rule then raises it to 1,000 °C. Smoke keeps the temperature of the fire it came from, so it can heat oil it touches past 250 °C. Smoke and steam in open air cool toward the air's temperature (see Heat and the air); steam cools toward its condensing point but condenses in practice only against cold solids (backlog I25).

Scenes (`?scene=`): `iceLava` (a 10×10 ice block against a 30×8 lava pool: lava is thick, so it creeps toward the ice; the first water appears after about 100 supersteps and the first steam after about 1,000), `steamCeiling` (steam under a −50 °C metal ceiling condenses; at 150 °C it does not; the ceiling is eight rows thick under one row of steam, since condensing takes 2,257 MJ/m³) and `moltenMetal` (a pool at 1,600 °C on a metal floor sets back into metal; lava at 1,300 °C never melts metal).

### Heat and the air

Intuition: empty space is air, and air has a temperature. A hot block warms the air touching it, that air rises and carries the warmth up and away, and cooler air flows in beside it, so the block cools down in the open and a fire, a pool of lava or a hot plate sends up a plume of hot air that draws smoke in toward its base. Steam in open air gives some heat to the air, but a steam cell holds the latent heat of a whole cell of water and the air cannot take it, so steam condenses mainly against cold solids (backlog I25). The warmth leaves through the edge of the world, where the air is always at room temperature; inside a box sealed by walls nothing leaves, and the total heat stays put.

The pieces, all in the air step (`src/shaders/air.wgsl.ts`, constants in `AIR`):

- **Exchange** (the exchange pass, once per call to `simulate` (a frame), before the CA, one thread per air cell): each cell that holds heat trades heat with an air cell through a surface coefficient, `AIR.surfaceCoefficient` in W/m²·K, the same for every material: the share of the way to the capacity-weighted mean that the pair moves in one superstep is `airShare` = PAIR_TIME · h / CELL_SIZE · (1 / C_cell + 1 / C_air cell), at most 1, with the capacities in MJ/m³·K (the air cell holds `AIR.heatCapacity` · s², 0.0012 per grid cell, so 0.019 per 4×4 air cell). The cell's side of the difference is `cellTemp` (its stored temperature clamped to its thresholds), so a cell inside a phase change trades at its threshold while its stored value moves. The heat C·T of cells and air is conserved up to rounding. A powder or liquid moving at `AIR.stillSpeed` (0.25 cells per dispatch) or faster trades no heat with the air, as a flyer: the exchange stands for a cell warming the still air beside it, and a cell crossing a fresh air cell every few dispatches would mix with a whole air cell of cold air each frame (a falling lava cell would lose about 160 °C a frame, and a poured stream would set in mid-fall and hang there as stone, since a solid never moves). A cell falling from rest passes that speed within 8 dispatches. The pass folds in the frame's supersteps: a pair that trades a share `g` per superstep trades `1 − (1 − g)^n` over `n` of them at once, what `n` supersteps leave of it if the air stands still between them. At the frame's own length the cooling of a block of metal is close to the same exchange at every superstep; at 8x it is slower, because the air carries the heat away between exchanges and meets fresh cells only once a frame.
- **Which air cell**: a powder, liquid, gas or fire cell trades with the air cell it is in. A solid that holds heat (metal, stone, ice) trades with the air cell of its first open neighbor, tried up, left, right, then down (open means empty space, a gas or fire), and a solid with no open neighbor trades none. The reason is the mask: an air cell holding only solid has every face closed, so the air over a 16×16 block of metal could never take its heat if the block's cells traded with the air cell they are in; an exposed surface trades with the air next to it, which is also what a surface does. The thread of an air cell therefore reads its cells and the ring of one cell around them to find the solids that belong to it, and each cell belongs to exactly one thread, so no two threads write the same cell. The cells are visited in a fixed order, so reruns are bit-identical.
- **Hot air rises** (the velocity pass): each top face gains `AIR.buoyancy` (2·10⁻⁶ cells per dispatch² per degree) times the mean temperature above room temperature of the two air cells it joins, times one less the share of those cells that powder or liquid fills (hot air inside a pile or a pool is not free to rise, and without that factor the hot air of a burning charge drew the sand in). Friction limits the speed: at most buoyancy · degrees / `velocityLoss`, 0.2 cells per dispatch at 500 °C above room temperature.
- **Heat moves with the air** (advect and diffuse, see The step): the flux form conserves the air's heat exactly, and diffusion spreads it across open faces.
- **The edge**: the ghost cells beyond the grid stay at 20 °C and the open faces carry heat out, so a hot world cools back to room temperature.

Air holds little heat: its volumetric heat capacity is 1.2 kJ/m³·K, about 1/3,500 of water's, `AIR.heatCapacity` = 0.0012 per grid cell on the elements' scale. A hot surface in still air cools at about 5 to 10 W/m²·K (natural convection): the exchange's surface coefficient is one link of two and the air's own removal of the heat (diffusion across air cells and the rising plume) the other, so `AIR.surfaceCoefficient` (40 W/m²·K) sits above the target: in series with the air's own removal of about 11 to 12 W/m²·K at the default air cell size of 4, the effective value is about 9, measured for a steel block 7 cm wide (M8.8 AC7). That removal depends on the air cell size, so the effective value does too. `AIR.buoyancy` stays M6's, tuned against the plume. The exchange does not scale with the air cell size except through the air cell's capacity, so at `?air=2` and `?air=1` (a quarter and a sixteenth of the capacity per air cell) heat moves between cells and air at other rates than at the default.

Example (`tests/air-heat.test.ts`, 240 supersteps a second at 1x):

- A 16×16 metal block at 1,000 °C in a 96×128 box open at the top cools to 944 °C in 30,000 supersteps (125 s, a time constant of about 35 minutes), and after 1,200 supersteps the air just above it is 364 to 375 °C warmer than the air at the box's side, for the block aligned with the air cells and shifted by 2. A steel block 50 cells (7 cm) wide at 500 °C cools at an effective 9 W/m²·K.
- 64 steam cells at 110 °C released 20 cells above the floor of an open box do not condense within 3,600 supersteps (15 s): a steam cell holds 2,257 MJ/m³ of latent heat and warming the air to 100 °C takes about 23,500 cells of it per steam cell. They have cooled to a mean of 96 °C, and with `air: false` they stay at 110 °C.
- The `plume` scene (a metal plate at 1,000 °C on the floor of a tall box open at the top, two smoke blocks beside it): after 960 supersteps the mean upward air speed over the plate is 0.055 cells per dispatch and that air is about 402 °C above room temperature. With the plate at 20 °C every air word stays unchanged.
- A sealed wall box holding every element on both sides of its thresholds keeps the total heat of cells, flyers and air, latent heat included, within 1·10⁻⁴ over 2,000 supersteps; 2,100 of its 4.2 million went into the air.

The plume carries smoke sideways, as inflow toward its base, but does not lift it faster than it rises alone: a gas that rose this dispatch drifts only sideways with the air (see Air moves matter), and smoke in open air already rises at the top speed of 1 cell per dispatch.

## Density by temperature

Intuition: a liquid expands when it is warm, so a warm blob in a cold tank is lighter than the water around it and rises, carrying its heat; cold water sinks. A gas does the same much more strongly, which is why hot smoke and steam shoot up and cool smoke spreads out. Water is odd: it is densest at 4 °C, so a pond under a freezing ceiling stirs until it is 4 °C throughout and only then freezes from the top.

The rows give the curve (`densityAt`, mirrored by `elDensityAt` in WGSL): a liquid with `densityKnots` interpolates linearly between up to four `[°C, kg/m³]` points and is flat outside them; a gas (and empty space) follows the ideal-gas law from its one density at `densityTemp`, ρ(T) = ρ_ref · (T_ref + 273.15) / (T + 273.15); a powder or solid has one density. A cell's temperature for this is `cellTemp`. Two different elements are ordered by their nominal densities as before (`canDisplace`, `canRiseThrough`): the temperature-aware compare cost the CA about 15% and no pair of the table crosses over, so only the cells of one element use the curve (backlog D34).

Both movements below are one swap with a chance from the relative density difference. Free convection speeds go as √(g · β · ΔT · L), the square root of the relative density difference, so the chance is `CONVECTION_GAIN` (2) · √((ρ_heavy − ρ_light) / ρ_light), at most 1 (`convectionChance`, `src/physics.ts`; WGSL twin in `ca.wgsl.ts`). The gain is the one number that sets the speed, tuned so that a block of water 70 K over its tank rises at the free-convection estimate.

### Convection

`convect`, part of phase 1: for each column of the block, when the two cells are the same liquid with a density curve and neither moved this dispatch, the upper swaps with the lower with the chance `convectionChance(ρ_upper, ρ_lower)` (0 unless the upper is denser). The cells keep their temperatures, so the swap carries heat; rising cells carry heat up and spreading carries it sideways, so no sideways swap is needed. A body at one temperature has no difference and stays still (`tests/convection.test.ts`).

Example: water at 20 °C is 996.5 kg/m³ and at 90 °C 964.0, so a 90 °C cell below 20 °C water is lighter by about 3% and swaps with the chance 2 · √0.034 = 0.37 per dispatch of its block. A 4×4 block of water at 90 °C in a 20 °C tank rises at 0.19 cells per dispatch against the estimate of 0.18 (gains 1 and 3 gave 0.08 and 0.34). A tank of 20 °C water on a 90 °C plate brings its top layer to half the plate's rise at least 10 times sooner than with flat density, and a tank at 10 °C under a −20 °C ceiling freezes first in its top quarter while its bottom water stays at or above 3.5 °C until the top row has frozen.

### Gas buoyancy

`buoyancy`, phase 0, for each column of the block: a gas cell lighter than the air with an empty cell above swaps with it with the chance `convectionChance(ρ_air, ρ_gas)`, and a gas heavier than the air with an empty cell below sinks by `convectionChance(ρ_gas, ρ_air)`. The air's density is `elDensityAt(EL_EMPTY, airTempAt(x, y))`, the air cell over the block's center (`airDensity`, read once for a block that holds a gas; room temperature without air). A gas rises through fire or a heavier liquid at once, whatever the chance. A gas at the air's temperature has no difference and stays where it is, so smoke floats while it is hot and spreads out once it has cooled to the air. Gases do not sort among themselves (backlog D35).

Example (`tests/buoyancy.test.ts`): smoke 5 °C over the air rises 25 cells per 100 supersteps (the test's bound is 45) and smoke at the air's temperature 0; a gas a twelfth heavier than the air falls 31 cells in 40 supersteps and a gas twice as heavy 79; steam at 110 °C and smoke at 150 °C rise a cell per dispatch (the chance is 1 once the relative difference reaches 25%), the old top speed.

## Movement by velocity

Intuition: a grain you drop does not fall at one fixed speed, it speeds up. Each falling cell carries a speed; every dispatch gravity adds a little, and the cell moves one cell whenever its unspent movement adds up to a whole cell. A grain starting from rest moves about 1 cell in the first 5 supersteps and 7 in the first 11, then 1 cell per dispatch, then flies.

Example (`tests/simulation.test.ts`, "a sand grain falls from rest with its element gravity"): with gravity 1/32 a grain has fallen `sum over k of min(k/32, 1)` cells after its k-th dispatch (the first dispatch gives no gravity); the test compares that sum with the grain's cell plus its remainder within 0.05 cells for the 23 supersteps it stays in the grid.

Why a limit of 1 cell per dispatch inside the grid: a block has room for one move per axis. Faster cells would need to cross blocks, which a thread working on one block cannot do safely, so they leave the grid instead.

## Viscosity

Intuition: water runs to a flat sheet at once, oil a little slower, lava creeps. A thin layer of liquid of depth h spreads under its own weight at about ρ · g · h² / (3μ) (a gravity current on a floor, μ the dynamic viscosity in Pa·s), so a thousand times the viscosity is a thousand times slower. The grid does not track a liquid's depth, so every liquid spreads as a layer `REF_DEPTH` (4) cells, 5.4 mm, deep, and the speed in cells per dispatch is that speed times `DISPATCH_TIME` / `CELL_SIZE` (`spreadSpeed`, `src/physics.ts`). A cell moves at most one cell per dispatch, so the speed is capped at 1, which makes it a chance: the row's `spread` word is min(1, speed), and a liquid cell makes a sideways move that its velocity does not carry (the spread along a row, and the slide down to a free bottom corner when it rests on something) only with that chance (`viscousGo` in `ca.wgsl.ts`). The slide must be gated too: without it the cells stacked on a lava column slid off its edge at full speed and the front ignored the viscosity. The turn-around draw (one move in 64) happens only on a move that would be made, so a thick liquid keeps a mean free path of 64 cells rather than 64 dispatches. A liquid without a viscosity spreads at the cap, and for water (spread 1) both are bit-identical to no gate at all.

Example (`tests/viscosity.test.ts`): water at 1 mPa·s is far above the cap (spread 1), molten metal (6 mPa·s) too, oil (0.2 Pa·s) has 0.67, lava (30 Pa·s) 0.013, about 6 cells a second at 1x. Dam-break fronts in the test: water 0.97 cells per dispatch, oil 0.44, lava 0.0076 (0.57 of its layer speed, within the factor of 3 the test allows, and oil between lava and water). `REF_DEPTH` is the one constant that sets how much a thick liquid creeps: at 3 cells and 100 Pa·s lava froze into a mound; at 4 and 30 oil stays below water and lava still creeps.

## Angle of repose

Intuition: a heap of grains stands at a slope that depends on how the grains catch each other: dry sand about 34°, smooth black powder flatter. In a grid, a grain resting on something with a free cell diagonally below it slides there, and a step of one cell is stable, so a pile of such grains would stand at 45°, one cell of height per column. A grain that is given a sideways speed as it slides carries on over the steps below until the floor's friction stops it, which flattens the pile.

A powder row's `repose` (degrees, 20 to 45, `slideSpeed` in `src/physics.ts`) gives that speed in cells per dispatch: v = √((45 − repose) / `REPOSE_FIT`), `REPOSE_FIT` = 80. It is the fit of 4,000 grains poured from a point at speeds 0.05 to 0.6, whose pile angle is about 45° − 80° · v², within 3° from 20° to 45°. The row's `slide` word holds it; a powder with no `repose` has speed 0 and is bit-identical to before. The slide is in phase 1 (the opposite-bottom-corner move of `velocityRule`): the grain leaves the step with `v.x = ±slide` in the direction of the slide, and friction (`MOVEMENT.friction`) and the rest speed stop it. A resting grain with a one-cell step beside it does not slide (static friction). A repose outside 20 to 45 is rejected, and only powders take one.

Example (`tests/repose.test.ts`, 4,000 grains poured from a point, no air, seed 1): sand 33.4° (data 34°), gunpowder 29.1° (30°, an estimate), a test powder at 25° stands at 24.2° and one at 45° at 44.4°; a powder with no repose stands at 44 to 45°.

## Flyers

A cell whose speed passes `launchSpeed` (1.5 cells per dispatch) leaves the grid as a flyer: a particle with a float position and velocity that carries the whole cell (every field) and flies under its element's `gravity` and `drag` (speed at most 16); with air it also feels the air, see Air moves matter. Intuition: debris thrown by a blast is too fast for the grid, so it travels as a free particle and lands back as a cell. Example: the `blast` scene throws sand to a 99th-percentile distance of 237 cells from the blast center, against 37 when every cell stays in the grid (`tests/simulation.test.ts`, "the blast throws debris as far as the prototype did").

The passes run once per superstep, after the offset-1 dispatch (`Flyers.recordSuperstep`):

1. **Launch**: the CA counts, per workgroup, the cells above the launch speed. One workgroup turns the counts into slots after the live flyers (a prefix sum), and each launching cell moves into its slot in the order cell index gives. Past `maxFlyers` the cell stays in the grid, its speed clamped to 1 cell per dispatch, and `capped` counts it.
2. **Flight**: two dispatches of gravity and drag (relative to the air, with the air's pressure gradient pushing, when there is air). A flyer walks the grid cells between its old and new position (4-connected, so it cannot slip between two diagonal cells) and stops in the last free cell before the first occupied one, so a wall one cell thick cannot be crossed. A flyer that stops, or slows below the launch speed, claims the free cell it is in with an atomic minimum on a key made of (cells walked, slot).
3. **Landing, two rounds**: the lowest key of each cell enters the grid with its speed clamped to 1 cell per dispatch and an empty remainder. A flyer that lost looks for a free cell within 3 cells, picked at random so that flyers which wanted the same cell spread out (one buried in the grid searches around the first free cell above it), and tries again in the second round; one that fails stays in the air and counts a retry (`jammed` counts 8 failures).
4. **Compaction**: a prefix sum over the survivors moves them to the other flyer buffer in order.

Every slot comes from a prefix sum in a fixed order and every conflict from the slot index, so two runs with one seed are bit-identical. The brush's erase and replace mark the flyers in the stroke as landed, and the next compaction drops them; Clear empties the list. The display reads an overlay buffer, cleared and filled once a frame with the kind word of each flyer's cell, and draws a flyer where the cell under it is empty.

## Brush

Painting writes the selected element into empty cells within the brush radius of the segment from the previous frame's pointer position to the current one (or into any non-wall cell when Shift is held); erasing empties every cell in range. The pass runs once per frame before the CA steps, so no block ever sees a half-painted state. The radius is in cells, whatever the grid scale. The painted variant comes from the cell coordinates and a per-frame seed, the painted life from the element's `life.start` (0 without one), and the painted temperature from the element's `temperature`; a replaced cell's temperature goes with the rest of it. When the stroke carries a temperature (`SimulationInput.temperature`, the picker's paint temperature field), the pass paints the element `paintTarget` (`src/toolbar.ts`) gives for the selected element at that temperature, with its ctype, at that temperature (words 10 and 11 of the brush uniform): from the row, `paintTarget` follows `above` while the temperature is over its threshold and `below` while it is under, never into fire, and stops at a threshold itself. Water at −10 °C paints ice, water at 110 °C steam, basalt at 1,300 °C lava, steel at 1,600 °C molten metal with ctype steel. The cell is painted at that stored temperature, with no latent heat taken in yet.

### Heat, Cool and Wind tools

With the Heat or Cool tool selected, the same brush stroke adds or takes `HEAT_TOOL_RATE` (17 °C, about 1,000 °C a second) per frame from the stored temperature of every cell that holds heat inside it, kept within −273.15 to 9,999 °C (`SimulationInput.heating`, word 9 of the brush uniform). Empty cells, walls and flyers are untouched; the right button still erases. A cell pushed past a threshold changes element on the next dispatch: it holds heat, so its block is never skipped.

The Wind tool (`SimulationInput.wind`, `AirGrid.recordWind`) paints nothing and has its own compute pass, recorded once per frame after the mask pass and before the CA. A stroke of `d` grid cells over a frame of `n` dispatches sets a target air speed along the stroke of `AIR.windToolGain · d / n` (0.5; capped at `maxSpeed`), and each face whose center is within the brush radius (at least one air cell) of the stroke closes `AIR.windToolBlend` (half) of the gap to it, except a closed face, which stays at 0. A faster drag blows harder (at 1x, 8 dispatches a frame, 10 cells a frame gives about 0.6 cells per dispatch and the cap is reached at 32); a pointer that did not move records no pass and leaves the air untouched, bit for bit; the right button still erases. Example (`tests/air-wind.test.ts`): 8 frames of 10 cells dragged rightward through a smoke cloud of radius 40 move its centroid 27 cells right, against 0.06 with no stroke. A gust is not carried by the air once the stroke ends: it spreads as pressure and fades, and 800 supersteps after a stroke swung left and right at the top speed every face speed is under 0.02 cells per dispatch and every |pressure| under 0.01, at s = 4 and 2, over empty space and over a sand floor.

## Seeing heat

Three views of the same `temp` words (`src/heat.ts`, `src/shaders/heat.wgsl.ts`, `src/shaders/display.wgsl.ts`), and a fourth of the air (see Seeing the air):

- **Glow** (normal view): a cell that holds heat and is hotter than 525 °C adds a glow color to its own, dull red at 525 °C, orange at 1,000 °C, yellow-white from 1,600 °C, at a weight that grows to full at 2,000 °C. Adding never darkens a light base color. An element with its own `graphics` function (fire) draws itself and gets no glow.
- **Heat view** (the "Heat view" button, or H; Ctrl, Meta and Alt ignore it): each cell that holds heat is drawn in the color of its temperature (`cellTemp`) from `HEAT_RAMP`, empty space in the color of its air's temperature at half brightness (the background without air) and walls at 30% of their color. Brightness rises with temperature at every step of the ramp, from dark blue at −50 °C through purple (20 °C), red (300 °C), orange (800 °C) and yellow (1,500 °C) to near white at 2,000 °C, so the temperature order never depends on hue alone. A legend above the toolbar labels −50, 100, 500, 1,000 and 2,000 °C.
- **Readout**: the toolbar shows the element and the whole-degree temperature of the cell under the pointer, then the pressure of its air cell (`Sand · 25 °C · pressure +0.20`); empty space shows the temperature of its air; a wall shows its label alone. `Simulation.probe` copies that cell's `kind` and `temp` words and, with air, the pressure and temperature words of its air cell into a staging buffer at the end of the frame's encoder (no extra submit), and `main.ts` reads it about every 100 ms and writes the line with `formatProbe` (`src/readout.ts`). Without air the line ends after the temperature.

A flyer is drawn over an empty cell from the two words of the overlay (`kind` and `temp`, written by one thread; two flyers over one cell may mix for a frame, display only), so a flying cell glows and takes its heat color in the heat view like a resting one.

**Halo** (finished look only, `src/glow.ts`): the cells that radiate also light the space around them. A coverage pass writes the light of every cell (`cellEmission`, `src/shaders/light.wgsl.ts`) into a half-resolution HDR texture: a cell that holds heat radiates its glow color times the fourth power of its absolute temperature counted from 525 °C and scaled to 1 at 1,000 °C (`emissionShare`), fire its graphics color twice as bright while young and fading with its life, a flyer by its own temperature. A chain of four downsamples (13 taps, Karis average on the first) and tent upsamples blurs it, and the composite adds the result times `LOOK.glowStrength` to every pixel but those of the cells that radiate themselves, which keep their own color. The cells look and the views record none of it.

**Clouds** (finished look only): gas cells (smoke, steam) are not drawn as cells but as a soft layer over the wall, its strength at a pixel the share of gas cells within 2 cells of it (weighted by a tent, so thin smoke stays wisps of its own shape rather than a blur), its color the mean color of the gas around from the coverage pass's gas texture (the colors the cells look draws, glow included), times `LOOK.gasOpacity`; a hot gas cell also takes the halo. `gasOpacity` 0 draws gas as cells; the cells look and the views always do. `docs/rendering.md` has the details.

**Liquid depth** (finished look only): a liquid whose row has an `absorption` (water, oil) is tinted by the depth of liquid above it, `exp(-absorption × depth in cells × LOOK.depthScale)` in linear light, the depth counted from the cells above the pixel in its own 4×4 texel and the liquid share of the cover texels above (the green channel, 32 texels at most). Water's absorption is Pope and Fry's plus an estimated turbidity, so deep water turns dark navy; the scale exaggerates it, since 128 cells are 17 cm. A pool's top row keeps its table color. A liquid without an absorption (lava, molten metal) is drawn flat, and the cells look draws every liquid as its table color. `docs/rendering.md` has the details.

**Air shimmer** (finished look with air only; `warpedUv` in `src/shaders/display.wgsl.ts`): the composite reads the picture (cells and textures) at a point shifted by the air at the pixel. Air more than 1 °C above room temperature shifts it by a smooth noise that rises at 12 cells per second, up to `LOOK.shimmer` (1.5) cells at 500 °C above room temperature; the pressure gradient shifts it along the gradient by `LOOK.shimmerPressure` (10) cells per unit of pressure per cell, about 2 cells across a blast's front; the total is capped at `LOOK.shimmerMax` (3) cells. Air at rest shifts nothing, exactly (the picture is then bit-identical to the one without shimmer). The cells look, the heat and air views and a simulation without air (no shimmer code is generated) are not shifted. `DisplayOptions.time` (seconds) animates the noise.

## Seeing the air

The air view (the "Air view" button, or A; Ctrl, Meta and Alt ignore it; it turns the heat view off and the heat view turns it off) draws the pressure of each air cell in `PRESSURE_RAMP` (`src/air.ts`) from −`AIR.viewRange` to +`AIR.viewRange` (±0.5): dark blue, blue, mid grey at 0 (ambient), khaki and pale yellow, each lighter than the one before, so brightness alone orders the pressures and no hue carries the meaning. The materials are drawn at 30% over it. The wind is drawn as arrows, one per 16×16 screen pixels (`AIR_VIEW`), pointing downwind, light with a dark outline, `11 · (speed / maxSpeed)^0.4` pixels long (a weak wind of 0.08 cells per dispatch is 3 pixels, a gale of 0.5 is 6), none under 2 pixels. A legend above the toolbar labels −0.5, 0 and +0.5. The stone crush limit (0.45) is 90% of the way up the ramp, and a gunpowder charge saturates it. The display reads the air state at binding 1 of its group 0 (`Display` takes the `AirGrid`); without air the view shows ambient pressure and no wind.

## Air

Intuition: empty space is air, and air is not nothing: squeeze it somewhere and the squeeze spreads out as a ring, like a ripple on a pond, and a wall stops the ripple. The air lives on its own coarse grid, much smaller than the cell grid (one air cell covers 4×4 cells by default), because a pressure wave does not need the detail of a grain of sand. It is stepped after the flyers of each superstep. The CA and the flyers read it (see Air moves matter), and the CA puts pressure into it when gunpowder burns or water flashes into steam (see Bursts), and the exchange pass trades heat between it and the cells that hold heat (see Heat and the air). Code: `src/air.ts` (the constants `AIR`), `src/air-grid.ts` (`AirGrid`: buffers, passes, `load`, `clear`, `read`), `src/shaders/air.wgsl.ts` (the passes).

### State

An air cell is `cellSize × cellSize` cells (`airCellSize`: 1, 2, 4 or 8, default 4; `?air=<size>` in the URL, or the `airCellSize` option of `Simulation`), `ceil(W / s) × ceil(H / s)` of them. One `vec4f` per air cell holds the speed of the air through its right face (x) and its top face (y), its pressure at its center (z) and its temperature in °C (w). Putting the speeds on the faces and the pressure in the middle (a staggered grid) keeps the two from drifting apart cell by cell into a checkerboard. Pressure is dimensionless, 0 is ambient. Speeds and `AIR` constants are in cells and dispatches, not air cells, so a different `s` changes the detail of the air and not its speeds. Everything starts, and is reset by `Simulation.load` and `clear`, at rest: pressure 0, speed 0, `ROOM_TEMP`. With `air: false` no buffer is allocated and no pass recorded.

The arrays hold one more column and row than there are air cells: position (a, b) is air cell (a − 1, b − 1), and column 0 and row 0 are ghost cells at pressure 0 and room temperature whose faces are the left and bottom faces of the first real cells, the edge of the world. Beyond the right and top edge the air is read as pressure 0 at room temperature.

### The step

The mask pass runs once per frame, after the brush: for each face it stores the face's openness, and for each air cell the share of powder and liquid cells and the count of cells that hold heat (the velocity pass reads the share; nothing reads the count). The exchange pass follows it, once (see Heat and the air). Then, after the flyer passes of each superstep, `substeps` air steps run, each of four passes in the frame's one compute pass:

1. **Velocity**: each face gains the pressure difference across it (the pressure with the CA's pending burst added: see Bursts) (and, on a top face, the buoyancy of the warm air, see Heat and the air), loses a share (`velocityLoss`) and, for the matter in its air cells, a further share (see Air moves matter), is clamped to `maxSpeed` and multiplied by the face's gate (see Walls). Per air step it gains `waveSpeed² · dt / s` per unit of pressure difference, with `dt = 2 / substeps` dispatches per step.
2. **Pressure**: the CA's burst for the cell (see Bursts) is added and cleared, the pressure falls by the net flow out of the cell times `dt / s`, then loses a share (`pressureLoss`).
3. **Advect**: the temperature is carried along with the air; the speeds and pressures are not (the air is a linear wave: a gust spreads as pressure and fades instead of coasting downwind on its own). The temperature crosses faces in flux form, so the heat the air carries is conserved exactly: what leaves a cell enters its neighbor, taken from the upwind side, as the excess over room temperature (so uniform room-temperature air stays put even where it is squeezed). The speed that carries heat is the air's up to `fluxCap = min(maxSpeed, s · substeps / 8)`, the speed at which a cell would hand over all its heat in one step; above it the upwind sum could overshoot.
4. **Diffuse**: the temperature spreads across the open faces (`diffusion`, 2% of the difference per dispatch), and the speeds and pressures are copied back.

Velocity and pressure update in place: the velocity pass writes only speeds and reads only pressures, the pressure pass the reverse. Advect writes the scratch buffer and diffuse copies it back, so every air step ends with the state in the buffer it started in and no buffer parity needs tracking.

The pair of velocity and pressure passes is a wave equation: a pulse spreads as a ring at `waveSpeed` (1 cell per dispatch, measured 1.02 times that at s = 4 and 1.05 at s = 2, the same in the four directions). A wave may cross at most 0.7 air cells per air step and the fastest air, which carries heat, at most one, which fixes `substeps = max(1, ceil(2 · waveSpeed / (0.7 · s)), ceil(2 · maxSpeed / s))`: 1 at s = 4 and 8, 2 at 2, 4 at 1.

Example: at s = 4 one air step is one superstep, two dispatches, so the front of a pulse moves 2 cells, half an air cell, per step and reaches the next air cell after 2 steps (`tests/air.test.ts`, AC3 measures the ring's radius against `waveSpeed`).

### Walls and edges

A face is closed by solid cells. The mask pass scans the rows of cells between the centers of the face's two air cells (the columns, for a top face): a row holding a solid cell is closed, and the face's openness is the share of open rows. A wall one cell thick closes every row it crosses at any angle, which a share-of-solid-cells rule would not (a one-cell wall fills a quarter of a 4×4 air cell). A diagonal wall crossing the corner of an air cell still leaves two faces half open, so a face's speed is multiplied by `faceGate = max(0, 2 · openness − 1)` rather than by the openness: a face open on at least half its rows stays a passage, and one open on less is shut, so no path goes through a one-cell wall. In the tests the far side of a one-cell wall, vertical, horizontal or diagonal, stays under 0.03% of the pulse's peak, and a gap of 3 air cells lets 12 to 16% through. Cells outside the grid count as open in the scan, so a wall drawn along the edge seals it.

The world's edge is open air: the faces on it carry no pressure gradient (for the matter too: the sampled gradient is 0 on them, so a plume leaving through the top does not push grains against the ceiling and the inflow at the floor does not lift them), and their speed is capped at `maxSpeed` like every other face, and the air through them leaves at `waveSpeed` times the pressure inside, which lets a wave out instead of bouncing it back inverted. The outermost ring of air cells also loses `edgeDamping` (2% per dispatch) of its pressure and speed, which takes the echo of waves that arrive at a slant (more damping reflects more: the ring then acts as a wall). The echo is under 30% of the wave that arrived at the worst point (a slanted arrival near a corner; 17% at s = 4, 13% at s = 2), and the pressure in the grid has fallen under 5% of a pulse's start within 65 supersteps (`tests/air.test.ts`, AC4). Heat leaves through the edge the same way: the ghost cells stay at room temperature.

### Air moves matter

Intuition: matter sits in the air and is carried and shoved by it. Wind drags a grain toward the wind's speed, and a pressure wave shoves it away from the high pressure, light stuff harder than heavy. Wind does not blow through a pile or a pool, and flames and smoke lean with the wind like anything else that floats.

The CA samples the air once per block, at the block's center, and only for a block that holds a powder, liquid, gas or fire cell (`airAround` in `src/shaders/air.wgsl.ts`, shared with the flight pass): the air's velocity there, bilinear from the faces, and the pressure gradient, in cells per dispatch. The air state is written only by the air passes, between CA dispatches, so this one read outside the block cannot race with the CA (`tests/block-locality.test.ts` binds a random air state and still requires bit-identical results outside the changed blocks). It is binding 1 of the CA's group 3.

- **Drag** (powders and liquids, in `velocityRule` right after gravity): the cell moves its velocity toward the air's by its element's `drag`, `v += drag · (air − v)`, so `drag` is the share of its speed relative to the air that it loses per dispatch. In still air this is the plain `v −= drag · v` a flyer has always had. Example: a grain falling in still air gains `gravity`, then loses `drag` of its speed, each dispatch (`tests/air-matter.test.ts`: its fall stays within 0.05 cells of that recurrence over 23 supersteps). Wind makes a grain lean downwind (the same test file): a thrown grain lands 30 cells downwind of its still-air landing in a steady wind of 0.5 cells per dispatch, and 30 cells upwind in a headwind.
- **Push** (the same step): `v −= push · gradient / density`, with `AIR.push` 16000. Drag alone cannot both let sand fly far (a small drag) and let wind throw it (a large one: advection-only coupling, with a drag strong enough to throw sand, makes sand fall like snow), so the pressure gradient pushes on its own, and lighter matter is pushed harder (the gradient is 0 on the world's edge faces, see Walls and edges). Dense sand can move only at its free surface, so a pulse of peak pressure 2 and width 6 cells, 10 cells under the surface of a 40-deep layer, lifts the surface grains out and 86% of the grains that move go away from the pulse's center (`tests/air-matter.test.ts`, measured at 8 supersteps; later the sand around falls back into the pit).
- **Flyers**: the flight pass samples the air at the flyer's position before each of its two dispatches per superstep and applies the same drag and push. Its drag is written `vel · (1 − drag) + drag · air`, which rounds like the flight without air, so in air at rest every flyer word is bit-identical to a run with `air: false`. The flight pass binds the CA's group 3, so it reads the same air buffer.
- **Gases and fire drift** (`driftCell`, a step after the sideways dispersion): a gas or fire cell moves one cell along each axis of the air's velocity, each axis with probability twice the speed along it (certain from half a cell per dispatch), when the cell it moves to is inside the block and empty. It tries the diagonal, then the vertical, then the horizontal move. It keeps no state, so `velocity` and `remainder` stay 0 for anything that does not fall, and in air at rest nothing moves. A cell that has already moved this dispatch (smoke that rose, which is every dispatch in open air) still drifts sideways but not vertically, which would undo its rise. Smoke in air held at a steady sideways speed moves at 0.70 to 0.73 of that speed (`tests/air-matter.test.ts`); gases still rise and spread by their own rules, the air adds a drift on top.
- **Matter slows the air** (the mask pass and the velocity pass): the mask stores the share of powder and liquid cells in each air cell, and a face loses a further `matterLoss` (5% per dispatch) of its speed times the mean of the shares of the two air cells it joins. The air in the middle of a pool or a pile therefore keeps 2% of the wind above it after 30 supersteps.

### Bursts

Intuition: a lit charge of gunpowder, or a drop of water that flashes into steam, pushes the air around it outward; the air then carries the push as a pressure wave (and throws sand, see above).

`Transition.burst` (0 or more, `above` transitions only; a `below` with a burst, a negative or non-finite one is rejected with an error naming the row) is the pressure a cell adds to its air cell when it passes that threshold. The CA releases it in two places: phase 8, when a cell passes any `above` threshold, and phase 6, when a cell ignites by touching fire and its element's `above` becomes fire (gunpowder; oil's burst is 0). The amount is added to the air cell's entry in the burst buffer (binding 2 of group 3) as `round(burst · AIR.burstScale)`, in 1/1024 units, with an integer `atomicAdd`, times `(AIR.burstCellSize / s)²`: the burst is the pressure for a 4×4 air cell, and the same gas raises a smaller air cell's pressure more, so a charge raises the pressure around it by the same amount at every air cell size. Integer additions commute, so the sum does not depend on the order the threads add in, and two runs with one seed stay bit-identical. The velocity pass reads the sum as part of the pressure and the pressure pass moves it into the pressure and clears the buffer. Without air the burst code is left out of the shader and the buffer does not exist.

Example (`tests/air-burst.test.ts`): gunpowder is 3, water 0.1. On the `explosion` scene (a 48-cell charge buried 12 cells deep in a 48-deep layer of sand on a wall shelf, a fire cell against its left side and a stone wall to its right) the sand within 28 cells of the charge gains a mean distance of 1.71 cells from the center in 8 supersteps (the grains the blast throws out land on the crater's slope and slide back toward the charge), and over 80% of the grains that move go outward; unlit, almost none move. At 3 the air is at its speed limit, so more burst only pushes the matter harder. For water, 160 cells poured onto lava raise the pressure around them to a peak of 0.052 and the wind to 0.063 cells per dispatch (water boils off lava at its real, slow pace), while one boiling cell barely stirs the air. A lit charge burns only part of itself; the rest is thrown unburnt (backlog I18).

### Pressure transitions

Intuition: a hard enough blast breaks rock. Basalt next to a gunpowder charge is crushed into gravel, brick into gravel, glass into broken glass and ice into snow, each out to a distance that grows as the material gets more brittle; the same material far away, or behind a wall, feels too little pressure and stays whole.

`ElementDef.pressureAbove` (`{ becomes }`; `becomes` an element of the table, and the chain of such transitions must not lead back to the row, or the table is rejected with an error naming the row) turns a cell into another element when the air pressure over it passes the limit. It is one way, instant and costs no latent heat. The pressure that crushes a row comes from its fracture toughness (`toughness`, MPa·√m: how easily a crack runs through the material): `PRESSURE_PER_TOUGHNESS` · toughness, one constant anchored so that stone, basalt's 1.7, crushes at the 0.45 `tests/air-crush.test.ts` tuned (a hand-given pressure is rejected). Toughness, not the tensile strength of flawless lab samples (where glass beats granite), decides what a blast breaks: every cell is the same size and carries flaws of about the same size, so the pressure that breaks it scales with its toughness. Glass (0.74) crushes at 0.20, brick (1.0, an estimate) at 0.26, ice (0.1) at 0.03, so a steam burst or boiling water breaks ice; ceramic (about 4) has no crush and no blast breaks it; metals, tens of times tougher, never crush. Phase 8 checks it after the temperature thresholds: the CA reads the pressure against the cell, the highest of the air cell over it and the air cells over its two neighbors inside the block that are not solid (`pressureAgainst`; the other two neighbors are inside the block on the next dispatch's offset), and when it is above the element's limit the cell is `retype`d to the target. Reading an open neighbor's air cell is what lets a blast break a wall from its surface: a wall 3 cells thick fills the air cells it crosses, which are closed to the air, so with its own air cell alone the stone in them would read no pressure, and a sealed charge would leave most of its wall standing. A solid neighbor, judged as phase 8 begins, is skipped: the air cell over a wall 1 cell thick may be open on the wall's far side, and the stone behind it must not feel that blast; the stone inside a thick wall feels the blast once the layer before it is crushed. The check exists only when the sim has air and the table has a pressure transition; the air state does not change during a dispatch and no cell outside the block is read, so the block-locality rule holds. An element with a pressure transition is never inert (`elementInert`), so its block is not skipped.

A crush keeps the cell's heat, not its temperature: the new cell starts at `stored temperature · C_old / C_new`, as at a threshold change, so the heat C·T of a sealed box is unchanged. The crushed forms (gravel, snow, broken glass) keep their parent's heat capacity, because a cell is one unit of matter whatever its form, so the ratio is 1 and the crushed cell keeps its temperature too: basalt at 500 °C becomes gravel at 500 °C, and the latent heats of both forms match their parent's. The loose bulk values of sand and gunpowder do not follow this convention (backlog I40).

Example (`tests/air-crush.test.ts`): a 48-cell charge on a platform in open air, with single stone cells every 3 cells to its right. `STONE_CRUSH` is 0.45: after 40 supersteps the farthest stone crushed is 16 cells from the charge's edge. The same row of single cells of brick, glass and ice breaks out to 28, 46 and 61 cells (the row's end), in the order of their toughness. The value sits above the 0.052 peak of water poured onto lava, so boiling water alone does not crush stone. Behind a wall 2 cells thick no stone is crushed, even at a limit of 0.03. A sealed charge breaks its walls: two rings of stone 3 cells thick with gunpowder between them lose 94% of their stone at s = 4 and all of it at s = 2 within 30 frames (AC29; the walls break open where they give first, and the rest of the blast vents there). A stone mass thicker than an air cell is crushed a layer at a time, as each crushed layer opens the air cells behind it.

### Thermal shock

Intuition: a hot glass dropped into cold water shatters because its surface shrinks while its inside has not yet, and the unequal stress splits it. A cell of a brittle solid cracks the same way when a neighbor in its block is much hotter or colder than it is; the more it expands for a degree and the stiffer it is, the less difference it takes, and the tougher it is, the more.

A row with `toughness` K, `expansion` α (1/°C) and `stiffness` E (GPa) has the shock limit ΔT = S · K · (1 − ν) / (E · α · 1000), with ν = 0.25 (Poisson's ratio varies little between these materials) and S one scale for every row, the strength per unit of toughness of a body with the flaw size every cell shares. S is anchored on soda-lime glass quenched in water at 20 °C, which cracks at a difference of 150 K (Malou et al. 2011; E 72 GPa, α 9·10⁻⁶), a flaw of about 10 µm (`shockLimit`, `GLASS_QUENCH` in `src/elements.ts`). The limits that follow: ice 26 K, glass 150 K, ceramic 174 K, basalt 620 K, brick 2,400 K. Metals and diamond have none and never crack; a powder is already broken. The limit is the row word `shockLimit` (0 for none) and the target the pressure target bits of `transitions`, which is the row's crush target, or `cracksInto` for a row with no crush (ceramic into gravel).

Phase 7 marks a cell (`cellCracks`) when the temperature of the cell and of its row neighbor or its column neighbor in the block (`cellTemp`, before the pairs exchange) differ by more than the limit and the neighbor holds heat. Phase 8 turns a marked cell that did not change at a threshold into its target, last, with its heat kept as a crush does, so a cell never melts and cracks in one dispatch. The diagonal is not compared, and the rule reads only the block, so `tests/block-locality.test.ts` still holds. Tests of other mechanics that put hot and cold solids side by side run on a table without limits (`tests/no-shock-table.ts`).

Example (`tests/shock.test.ts`, the `quench` scenario): a glass pane at 300 °C under a drip of water at 20 °C cracks at least 4 cells under the drip within 5 s, the same pane in still air none; an ice cell falling into water at 40 °C becomes snow; steel, aluminium and diamond never crack; a cold ceramic cup filled with steel at 1,600 °C cracks (74 of its 76 cells), a cup preheated to within its limit does not.

### Cost

On the RTX 4080 at 2560×1340 the air passes at 1x cost 0.07 to 0.12 ms at s = 8, 0.14 to 0.30 ms at s = 4 (default; measured with every pass in its own compute pass, which overstates the cost in the frame's one pass; the heat exchange is 0.06 to 0.15 ms of it, the most on the `thermal` scene), 0.8 to 0.95 ms at s = 2 and about 17 ms at s = 1 (four air steps per superstep over 3.4 million air cells), so 1×1 air is not usable at screen size. The figures at s = 8, 2 and 1 were measured before the heat exchange and are lower bounds. The whole cost of the air at 1x on the busy bench scenes, with the CA's air reads and the extra flyers, is +0.26 to +0.72 ms (+1.24 ms on `thermal`) over the engine alone (`docs/powder-toy.md`, "Frame budget"). `BENCH_AIR=off,8,4,2,1 npm run bench` measures them.

## Boundaries

`readCell` returns a wall for out-of-bounds coordinates, so the grid edge behaves like a wall, and writes outside the grid are skipped. No rule moves a wall, so out-of-bounds walls never enter the grid. Without fire in the scene every type keeps its count, flyers in the air included (every rule swaps cells); fire burns oil and ends as smoke.
