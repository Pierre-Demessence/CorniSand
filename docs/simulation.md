# Simulation

How a frame turns into particle movement. Code: `src/simulation.ts`, `src/elements.ts`, `src/element-table.ts`, `src/cell-fields.ts`, `src/cell-field-buffers.ts`, `src/shaders/cell.wgsl.ts`, `src/shaders/elements.wgsl.ts`, `src/shaders/ca.wgsl.ts`, `src/shaders/brush.wgsl.ts`, `src/movement.ts`, `src/flyers.ts`, `src/shaders/flyers.wgsl.ts`, `src/passes.ts`, `src/heat.ts`, `src/shaders/heat.wgsl.ts`, `src/air.ts`, `src/air-grid.ts`, `src/shaders/air.wgsl.ts`, `src/display.ts`, `src/toolbar.ts`.

## Frame pipeline

Each animation frame runs `stepsPerFrame` supersteps (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 24 or 32; the speed labelled "1x" is 4), then one render pass. All of it is recorded into one command encoder and sent with one `submit()` (`main.ts`). A frame is:

1. **Brush**: while a mouse button is down, the brush pass, one thread per cell (each thread touches only its own cell). Then the air's mask pass (see Air).
2. **Supersteps**: `stepsPerFrame` of them, each two CA dispatches with Margolus offset 0, then 1, then the flyer passes (see Flyers), then the air steps (see Air). The dispatches update the cell buffers in place. The brush, every CA dispatch and the flyer passes share one compute pass, handed out by `PassRecorder` (`src/passes.ts`); the bench gives each group of dispatches a timed pass of its own instead.
3. **Overlay and render**: the flyers are scattered into the overlay buffer, then the cell buffers and the overlay are drawn with the brush ring.

Every CA dispatch needs its own `{ offset, seed }`. They live in 256-byte slots (`minUniformBufferOffsetAlignment`) of one uniform buffer sized for the 64 dispatches of a 32-superstep frame; one `writeBuffer` fills the used slots and each dispatch selects its slot with a dynamic offset. The grid size is a shader constant.

At most every 2 seconds, on frames where no mouse button is down, a separate compute pass counts non-empty, non-wall cells with an atomic counter, adds the flyers in the air, and reads the total back asynchronously for the toolbar. It has its own submit.

## Cell format

A cell is one 32-bit word in each of the field buffers listed in `CELL_FIELDS` (`src/cell-fields.ts`), one storage buffer per field (`src/cell-field-buffers.ts`):

- `kind`: bits 0–7 `type`, the element ID (a row of the element table below); bits 8–15 `variant`, random 0–255 assigned at paint time (not used by the display yet); bits 16–31 reserved.
- `life`: for an element with a lifetime (fire), the time left. For a liquid (water, oil), the sideways direction the cell last moved in (0 none, 1 left, 2 right).

- `velocity`: the cell's velocity in cells per dispatch, x in the low 16 bits and y in the high 16 bits, each a binary16 float (`pack2x16float`). y = 0 is the bottom row, so a falling cell has a negative y.
- `remainder`: the part of the cell's movement it has not spent yet, packed the same way. A cell moves one cell inside its block when the remainder on an axis reaches 1, so a speed of 1/4 cell per dispatch is a move every four dispatches. Both words are 0 for anything that does not fall, and for a cell at rest.
- `temp`: the stored temperature in °C, an `f32` (the TypeScript side reads the words through `tempView`). It means nothing for a cell that holds no heat, empty space and walls: `makeCell`, the eraser and Clear leave it 0. A painted cell starts at its element's `temperature`, and so does every cell of a scene built by `src/scenes.ts` or loaded without a `temp` field (`Simulation.load`; a scene may set other temperatures, as `heatBars` does). Conduction (phase 7) and the phase changes (phase 8) change it for the cells that hold heat; a cell carries it unchanged wherever it moves or flies. For a cell inside a phase change it can lie past the element's threshold (see Phase changes).

The shaders see them as a `Cell` struct (`loadCell`, `storeCell`, `storeChanged`, `cellType`, `cellVariant`, `cellLife`, `cellVelocity`, `cellRemainder`, `cellStoredTemp`, `makeCell`, `withType`, `withLife`, `withMotion`, `withTemp`), generated from the field list with the bindings in `@group(1)`, binding = field index. A new field is one entry in `CELL_FIELDS`; `makeCell` starts it at 0, and the `with…` builders copy a cell and change only the named part. `storeChanged` compares an `f32` field by its bits, so a moved cell keeps its exact word (as floats, −0 equals 0).

Cells travel as whole structs: swapping two cells swaps every field.

Intuition for the layout: each field is its own array, like the columns of a table, so a pass can later skip the columns it does not need (today every pass binds all of them), and the device's buffer size limit applies to one column at a time. The CA updates the arrays in place instead of writing a second copy: a thread loads its block's four cells, runs the rules, and writes back only the fields that differ from what it loaded. This is safe because blocks do not overlap, so a rule must read and write only the four cells of its own block.

## Element table

Every material is a row of `ELEMENTS` (`src/elements.ts`):

| Element | ID | State | Density (kg/m³) | Other |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| empty | 0 | empty | 1.2 (air) | |
| sand | 1 | powder | 1600 | gravity 1/32; conductivity 0.05, capacity 1.3 |
| wall | 2 | solid | 2500 | |
| water | 3 | liquid | 1000 | gravity 1/32; conductivity 0.3, capacity 4.2; below 0 °C ice (latent 300), above 100 °C steam (latent 400) |
| smoke | 4 | gas | 0.6 | conductivity 0.05, capacity 1 (placeholder, backlog I16) |
| oil | 5 | liquid | 900 | flammable, gravity 1/32; conductivity 0.1, capacity 1.7; above 250 °C fire |
| fire | 6 | plasma | 0.3 | life 200, loses 4 per dispatch, becomes smoke; rule `fire`; graphics `fire`; 1,000 °C; conductivity 0.5, capacity 1 |
| metal | 7 | solid | 7870 | conductivity 1, capacity 3.5; above 1,538 °C molten metal (latent 600) |
| stone | 8 | solid | 2700 | conductivity 0.1, capacity 2.2; above 1,200 °C lava (latent 400); above air pressure 0.2 sand (see Pressure transitions) |
| ice | 9 | solid | 917 | painted at −20 °C; conductivity 0.2, capacity 1.9; above 0 °C water (latent 300) |
| steam | 10 | gas | 0.59 | painted at 110 °C; conductivity 0.2, capacity 2; below 100 °C water (latent 400) |
| lava | 11 | liquid | 2700 | gravity 1/32; painted at 1,300 °C; conductivity 0.15, capacity 2.6; below 1,200 °C stone (latent 400) |
| molten metal | 12 | liquid | 7000 | gravity 1/32; painted at 1,600 °C (not paintable from the toolbar); conductivity 0.6, capacity 4; below 1,538 °C metal (latent 600) |
| gunpowder | 13 | powder | 1700 | flammable, gravity 1/32; conductivity 0.03, capacity 1.4; above 427 °C fire (burst 3); denser than sand and water, so it sinks through both |

The states: *empty* is air; a *solid* never moves and nothing passes through it; *powders* and *liquids* fall, and liquids also spread sideways; *gases* rise; *plasma* (fire) stays where it is, but a gas rises through it. A row also holds a label, a color, a `temperature` (°C, default 20: what a painted or loaded cell starts at), a `conductivity` (0 to 1, default 0), a `heatCapacity` (above 0, default 1) and optional `below` and `above` transitions (a threshold in °C, the element it becomes and its latent heat; see Heat; an `above` also takes a `burst`, see Bursts), an optional `pressureAbove` (an air pressure and the element beyond it, see Pressure transitions), an optional `rule`, an optional `graphics` function and an optional toolbar order. A powder or liquid row has a `gravity` (cells per dispatch squared, default 1/32) and a `drag` (the share of its speed relative to the air it loses per dispatch, in the grid and in flight, default 1/64; see Air moves matter); other states have no gravity.

An element *holds heat* when its conductivity is above 0 or it has a transition (`elementHoldsHeat`, the table's `holdsHeat` word): its cells' `temp` words mean something. Empty space and the wall hold none. WALL is the engine's invincible wall and the world's edge, not a real material: it never conducts, so heat never leaves the world; the real building materials are metal and stone. SMOKE is a placeholder for the smoke of every fire, and its heat values are too (backlog I16). Every element models a real material with physical properties; one that does not says so in its row.

The CA, the brush and the display read the table as a uniform array of 256 rows of 96 bytes (a uniform array's stride is a multiple of 16) (`@group(2)`; `ElementTable` fills it, `PROP_FIELDS` lists a row's words and generates both the WGSL struct and the packer). A row is 24 words: the color, density, state, flammable flag, life start and rule, gravity, drag, the derived `inert` and `holdsHeat` flags, temperature, conductivity and heat capacity, then the transition words: `belowTemp`, `belowWidth`, `aboveTemp`, `aboveWidth`, `transitions` (the element ID each transition leads to, in 8 bits each: below in bits 0–7, above in 8–15, the pressure transition in 16–23), `aboveBurst` and `pressureAbove` (an `f32`, `NO_PRESSURE` = 3·10³⁸ when the element has none: a finite value that no pressure reaches, since infinity may not compare the same on every GPU), and two words of padding. A uniform array indexed by the cell's type is one cached read, where a `const` array may be copied into every thread's memory. The element IDs and states are also generated as WGSL constants (`EL_SAND`, `ST_POWDER`), so no shader names a number. A test or a variant of the game gives `Simulation` another table (and extra rule sources) through its options.

Intuition: a new material only has to say what it is, powder or liquid or gas, and how heavy it is; the CA then knows it falls, piles up, sinks through lighter things and floats on heavier ones. Example: a powder row with density 1300 (mud) falls like sand, sinks through water (1000) and stays on top of sand (1600) with no other change (`tests/element-table.test.ts`).

## Margolus neighborhood

Intuition: the grid is cut into 2×2 blocks, and each block is updated on its own, so two particles can never try to move into the same cell. On the next step the block grid shifts by one cell diagonally, so particles cross block borders over time.

Example with offset 0, the block whose bottom-left cell is (4, 6) holds (4, 6), (5, 6), (4, 7), (5, 7). With offset 1 the cell (4, 6) belongs instead to the block starting at (3, 5), as its top-right cell.

One thread handles one block: it reads the four cells, runs all rules and writes back the changed fields. Block `b` has its bottom-left cell at `2·b − offset`, and there are `ceil((W + offset) / 2) × ceil((H + offset) / 2)` blocks, so at offset 1 (and at odd sizes) edge blocks stick out of the grid.

A block whose four cells are all inert (empty or solid, with no lifetime, no rule, no heat and no pressure transition: `elementInert`, the table's `inert` word) cannot change in any phase, so the thread reads only their `kind` words and stops. Most of a screen is empty space or walls, so most threads stop there. A thread keeps its block in four separate variables, not in an array: on the RTX 4080 an array indexed by a slot computed at run time sits in slow per-thread memory instead of registers, which made the CA about three times slower.

y = 0 is the bottom row; gravity points toward lower y.

Smoke rising from a bottom row moves one cell per dispatch, two per superstep. Falling cells start slower and speed up (see phase 1).

## Randomness

`pcg()` is the PCG-RXS-M-XS 32-bit hash. A block's random word is `pcg(bx ^ pcg(by ^ seed))`, with `bx`, `by` the block indices and `seed` a `u32` from `crypto.getRandomValues`, drawn per dispatch. Later random choices in the same block chain `pcg()` on the previous word. A choice between two options uses the top bit.

## Rules inside a block

Phases run in order on the block's four cells (BL, BR, TL, TR), each phase seeing the previous phase's result. Movement depends on the table's `state` and `density`, and for powders and liquids on the cell's velocity. With air, a block that holds a powder, liquid, gas or fire cell first samples the air once, at the block's center (see Air moves matter); a block without one skips the sample.

0. **Gases rise**: a bottom gas cell swaps with the cell above if that is empty, plasma (fire), or a liquid heavier than the gas (smoke through water and oil).
1. **Falling cells move by velocity** (`velocityRule`, the bottom row first, the column order random): a powder or liquid cell adds its element's `gravity` to its vertical speed when it is in the top row or already moving (a cell at rest starts falling only once a dispatch has seen the cell below it free, or every level of a resting stack would bank one more step than the one under it and the top of a deep layer would launch), with air then loses `drag` of its speed relative to the air and is pushed by the air's pressure gradient (see Air moves matter), adds its velocity, at most 1 cell per axis, to its remainder, and when the remainder reaches 1 on an axis swaps with the cell in the way if `canDisplace` allows it: the cell is empty, or a gas, powder or liquid lighter than the mover. So sand (1600) sinks through water (1000) and oil (900), water through oil, and the displaced cell moves up. A cell never displaces one of its own density, and nothing displaces a solid or fire. A blocked axis loses its speed, except that a cell blocked by a slower cell moving the same way takes that cell's speed, so a falling column falls together. A cell faster than 1.5 cells per dispatch does not move in the grid; the flyer passes take it. Resting and sliding are part of the same phase (`velocityRule`): a powder or liquid cell resting on something that does not give way loses a quarter of its sideways speed (below 1/256 it stops), and moves to the opposite bottom corner if `canDisplace` allows it. A settled pile ends with velocity and remainder exactly 0.
2. **Gases spread**: a top-row gas cell moves into the empty neighbor in a randomly picked direction (one try); a bottom-row one does too when it cannot rise. Only a cell that did not move earlier in the dispatch moves again. With air, a further step follows the dispersion: **gases and fire drift with the air** (see Air moves matter).
3. **Liquids spread** (`spreadRow`, top row then bottom row): a liquid cell that did not move earlier in the dispatch and is not falling moves into the empty cell beside it in its row, if that is the direction it remembers (a cell without memory picks one at random). The memory is the cell's `life` field: a cell that moved keeps going in that direction on later dispatches, which makes a stream flow instead of diffuse. A cell facing anything but empty space turns around, and one that could move turns around one time in 64 instead. Two liquids side by side never swap, so a layered body stays layered.
4. **Lifetimes**: an element with a `life` row loses its `decay` per dispatch and becomes the element named by `becomes` when it runs out (fire becomes smoke at 0).
5. **Element rules**: for each cell, a generated `switch` on its element calls that element's rule, if the row has one; the rule sees the block and may change any of its cells. Fire's rule: fire next to water becomes smoke; otherwise a burning cell is raised to at least fire's temperature (1,000 °C), so fire heats what it touches however it was lit.
6. **Ignition**: a flammable cell (oil) with a fire cell in its block becomes fire, starting at fire's `life.start`. Which cells ignite is decided right after the lifetimes (phase 4), before the rules, and applied here, so a rule never sees a cell that ignites in the same dispatch: oil that ignites beside water burns for a step before its fire goes out, while the fire that lit it is put out at once. Oil that is hot enough also becomes fire, as a transition (phase 8). With air, a cell whose element's `above` transition becomes fire (gunpowder) releases that transition's `burst` into its air cell as it ignites (see Bursts); oil's `burst` is 0, so it releases nothing.
7. **Heat conduction**: the four cells of the block exchange heat pairwise, see Heat.
8. **Phase changes**: a cell whose stored temperature has run past a threshold of its element, by the whole latent width, becomes the element beyond it, see Phase changes; with air, a cell that passes an `above` threshold also releases that transition's `burst` (see Bursts). After the temperature thresholds the phase checks the pressure of the cell's air cell against the element's `pressureAbove` and, past it, turns the cell into the element beyond it (see Pressure transitions). Without any transition in the table, or with only pressure transitions and no air, the phase is left out of the shader.

Fire has no movement rule; it only moves when a gas rises through it.

Intuition for the liquids: each cell looks only at its 2×2 block, so a liquid cannot feel the weight of the water above it or push water along a pipe. A pool flattens because surface cells slide off the high spots, and falls into every gap. Example: a 30×40 block of water against the left wall of a 200-wide tank spreads out to a sheet 6 to 7 cells deep in about 1,200 supersteps (`tests/simulation.test.ts`, "dam break"). Connected vessels do not level: in the `uTube` scene the filled arm stays full and the empty arm stays empty, as in The Powder Toy (`docs/decisions.md`).

## Heat

Intuition: a hot cell warms the cell next to it and cools down itself, until the two have the same temperature, and a metal passes heat on much more readily than stone. Every cell that holds heat has a temperature (`temp`); empty space and the wall hold no heat in the cell grid and stay out of the block exchange below. Empty space has the temperature of its air cell instead, and a cell that holds heat trades heat with that air (see Heat and the air), so a hot cell in open air cools down; in a box sealed by walls its heat stays inside.

Each dispatch, in phase 7 (after the rules and ignition, before the phase changes), the four cells of a block exchange heat in four pairs: the two rows, then the two columns at offset 0, the two columns, then the two rows at offset 1, so neither axis always goes first. Over a superstep every cell exchanges with each of its four neighbors. For a pair (a, b) with conductivities κa, κb and heat capacities Ca, Cb:

- the pair's conductivity is the harmonic mean g = 2·κa·κb / (κa + κb), and 0 when either κ is 0 (the plain formula is 0/0 for two cells that hold no heat, which would write NaN into empty cells);
- with d = Ta − Tb, the difference of the two cells' temperatures (`cellTemp`: the stored temperature clamped to the element's thresholds, see Phase changes), the stored Ta −= g·d·Cb / (Ca + Cb) and Tb += g·d·Ca / (Ca + Cb); nothing is written when g or d is 0, so a cell that holds no heat keeps its `temp` word bit for bit.

Because Ca·ΔTa + Cb·ΔTb = 0, the heat C·T is conserved up to rounding; each exchange moves both cells toward their capacity-weighted mean and never past it (the difference of their temperatures becomes d·(1 − g); a cell inside a phase change is at its threshold while its stored value moves), so any κ in [0, 1] and any capacities are stable. The exchange reads and writes only the block's cells, which `tests/block-locality.test.ts` checks. The capacity is per cell: a cell is one unit of matter whatever its state, so metal (3.5) takes 3.5 times the heat of a fresh cell of capacity 1 for the same change of temperature, and water (4.2) soaks up the most. Conductivity and capacity are tuned from physical ranks, not physical units; the comment on each row says where its numbers come from.

Speed: for one element, a unit of heat steps to its pair partner with probability κ/2 per dispatch, and its next partner is on the far side unless it stepped, so the variance of its position grows by κ/(2 − κ) cells² per dispatch along each axis. For metal (κ 1) that is 1 cell² per dispatch, a spread of about 22 cells in a second at 1x (62 at 8x); for stone (κ 0.1) about 5 (14). κ = 1 is the most a per-dispatch exchange allows.

Example (`tests/heat.test.ts`): a 4-column line at 500 °C in a 256-wide slab at 20 °C. Between superstep 100 and 400 the variance of the excess heat along the slab grows by 600·κ/(2 − κ) within 5% (measured within 0.01%), for metal and for stone. In the `heatBars` scene (a metal bar and a stone bar, 48×4 each, hot in the 6 left columns) the far end of the metal bar is 10 °C above its start after about 180 supersteps, the stone bar's after about 3,300.

### Phase changes

Intuition: ice at 0 °C does not melt the moment it gets a little heat; it sits at 0 °C and takes in a fixed amount of heat (the latent heat) first, and only then becomes water. A cell keeps that bookkeeping in its stored temperature: while it takes in its latent heat the stored temperature runs past the threshold, and the temperature it shows and conducts at stays at the threshold. Once the stored value has run past by the whole latent amount, the cell changes element.

Example: water has capacity 4.2 and boils at 100 °C with latent heat 400, so its latent width is 400 / 4.2 = 95 °C. Water stored at 140 is boiling water at 100 °C that has taken in 40·4.2 of the 400 it needs; stored at 196 it becomes steam. Heat that flows into it meanwhile raises the stored value and nothing else, and a neighbor sees a cell at 100 °C all along, so a boiling surface keeps drawing heat at the full rate until it has paid for the change.

The rule (`transitionCell` in `ca.wgsl.ts`, phase 8): with a threshold T, a latent heat L and a capacity C, the latent width is w = L / C. `cellTemp` is the stored temperature clamped to [below.temp, above.temp] of the element (an absent threshold is ±3e38, so the clamp needs no branch). A cell whose stored temperature s passes above.temp + w becomes the element above it, with the stored temperature T + (s − T − w) · C_old / C_new, and with the new element's life (`retype`); one under below.temp − w likewise downward. The heat C·s plus a per-element offset is the same before and after, so the change creates and destroys no energy; `latentOffsets` in `tests/metrics.ts` works the offsets out from the table, and `tests/heat.test.ts` checks the total of a box across every threshold between the paired elements over 2,000 supersteps (a relative change of 6e-8). A new cell starts at or past the threshold on the side it came from, so it does not turn back at once; a large overshoot can carry it through the next change in the same direction on the next dispatch (ice stored at 1,000 °C becomes water, then steam).

A pair that turns into each other (water and ice, water and steam, stone and lava, metal and molten metal) shares one threshold and one latent heat, and the table is rejected otherwise (`validateElements`). A cell has to give back a whole latent heat to change back, so a surface between steam and water does not flicker: `tests/heat.test.ts` packs a box with ice over water, and one with steam over water, and no cell changes element in 500 supersteps. Oil above 250 °C becomes fire (one way, no latent heat); the fire rule then raises it to 1,000 °C. Smoke keeps the temperature of the fire it came from, so it can heat oil it touches past 250 °C. Smoke and steam in open air cool down through the air (see Heat and the air), so steam condenses and smoke goes cold.

Scenes (`?scene=`): `iceLava` (a 10×10 ice block against a 30×8 lava pool: the cells touching the lava melt and boil within a few supersteps, the steam pushes the lava back and leaves a gap of empty space, which carries no heat, so about two thirds of the ice stay), `steamCeiling` (steam under a −50 °C metal ceiling condenses; at 150 °C it does not) and `moltenMetal` (a pool at 1,600 °C on a metal floor sets back into metal within about 15 supersteps; lava at 1,300 °C never melts metal).

### Heat and the air

Intuition: empty space is air, and air has a temperature. A hot block warms the air touching it, that air rises and carries the warmth up and away, and cooler air flows in beside it, so the block cools down in the open and a fire, a pool of lava or a hot plate sends up a plume of hot air that lifts smoke. Steam in open air gives its heat to the air and turns back into water. The warmth leaves through the edge of the world, where the air is always at room temperature; inside a box sealed by walls nothing leaves, and the total heat stays put.

The pieces, all in the air step (`src/shaders/air.wgsl.ts`, constants in `AIR`):

- **Exchange** (the coupling pass, once per air step, one thread per air cell): each cell that holds heat trades heat with an air cell by the pair rule of the block exchange (`exchangeHeat`): the conductivity is the harmonic mean of the cell's κ and `AIR.conductivity` (0.5), the capacities are the cell's and `AIR.heatCapacity` · s² (0.04 per grid cell, so 0.64 per 4×4 air cell), and both move toward their capacity-weighted mean without passing it. The cell's side of the difference is `cellTemp` (its stored temperature clamped to its thresholds), so a cell inside a phase change trades at its threshold while its stored value moves. The heat C·T of cells and air is conserved up to rounding.
- **Which air cell**: a powder, liquid, gas or fire cell trades with the air cell it is in. A solid that holds heat (metal, stone, ice) trades with the air cell of its first open neighbor, tried up, left, right, then down (open means empty space, a gas or fire), and a solid with no open neighbor trades none. The reason is the mask: an air cell holding only solid has every face closed, so the air over a 16×16 block of metal could never take its heat if the block's cells traded with the air cell they are in; an exposed surface trades with the air next to it, which is also what a surface does. The thread of an air cell therefore reads its cells and the ring of one cell around them to find the solids that belong to it, and each cell belongs to exactly one thread, so no two threads write the same cell. The cells are visited in a fixed order, so reruns are bit-identical.
- **Hot air rises** (the velocity pass): each top face gains `AIR.buoyancy` (2·10⁻⁶ cells per dispatch² per degree) times the mean temperature above room temperature of the two air cells it joins, times one less the share of those cells that powder or liquid fills (hot air inside a pile or a pool is not free to rise, and without that factor the hot air of a burning charge drew the sand in). Friction limits the speed: at most buoyancy · degrees / `velocityLoss`, 0.2 cells per dispatch at 500 °C above room temperature.
- **Heat moves with the air** (advect and diffuse, see The step): the flux form conserves the air's heat exactly, and diffusion spreads it across open faces.
- **The edge**: the ghost cells beyond the grid stay at 20 °C and the open faces carry heat out, so a hot world cools back to room temperature.

Air holds little heat: its volumetric heat capacity is about 1/3,500 of water's, 0.0012 per grid cell on the elements' scale. `AIR.heatCapacity` is 33 times that: with the physical value each air cell carries almost nothing away, and a 16×16 block of metal at 1,000 °C only reached 820 °C after 125 s. The capacity, `AIR.conductivity` and `AIR.buoyancy` trade against each other (a stronger plume pushes sand next to a large hot plate, backlog I19) and are tuned against the targets below. The exchange runs once per air step with the capacity of an air cell, with no scaling by the number of steps per superstep, so at `?air=2` and `?air=1` heat moves between cells and air at other rates than at the default.

Example (`tests/air-heat.test.ts`, 240 supersteps a second at 1x):

- A 16×16 metal block at 1,000 °C in a 96×128 box open at the top falls below 200 °C after about 19,500 supersteps (81 s), and after 1,200 supersteps the air just above it is 466 to 482 °C warmer than the air at the box's side, for the block aligned with the air cells and shifted by 2.
- 64 steam cells at 110 °C released 20 cells above the floor of an open box all condense within 840 supersteps (3.5 s); with `air: false` none do.
- The `plume` scene (a metal plate at 1,000 °C on the floor of a tall box open at the top, two smoke blocks beside it): after 960 supersteps the mean upward air speed over the plate is 0.077 cells per dispatch and that air is about 509 °C above room temperature. With the plate at 20 °C every air word stays unchanged.
- A sealed wall box holding every element on both sides of its thresholds keeps the total heat of cells, flyers and air, latent heat included, within 1·10⁻⁴ over 2,000 supersteps; 80,000 of its 4.2 million went into the air.

The plume carries smoke sideways, as inflow toward its base, but does not lift it faster than it rises alone: a gas that rose this dispatch drifts only sideways with the air (see Air moves matter), and smoke in open air already rises at the top speed of 1 cell per dispatch.

## Movement by velocity

Intuition: a grain you drop does not fall at one fixed speed, it speeds up. Each falling cell carries a speed; every dispatch gravity adds a little, and the cell moves one cell whenever its unspent movement adds up to a whole cell. A grain starting from rest moves about 1 cell in the first 5 supersteps and 7 in the first 11, then 1 cell per dispatch, then flies.

Example (`tests/simulation.test.ts`, "a sand grain falls from rest with its element gravity"): with gravity 1/32 a grain has fallen `sum over k of min(k/32, 1)` cells after its k-th dispatch (the first dispatch gives no gravity); the test compares that sum with the grain's cell plus its remainder within 0.05 cells for the 23 supersteps it stays in the grid.

Why a limit of 1 cell per dispatch inside the grid: a block has room for one move per axis. Faster cells would need to cross blocks, which a thread working on one block cannot do safely, so they leave the grid instead.

## Flyers

A cell whose speed passes `launchSpeed` (1.5 cells per dispatch) leaves the grid as a flyer: a particle with a float position and velocity that carries the whole cell (every field) and flies under its element's `gravity` and `drag` (speed at most 16); with air it also feels the air, see Air moves matter. Intuition: debris thrown by a blast is too fast for the grid, so it travels as a free particle and lands back as a cell. Example: the `blast` scene throws sand to a 99th-percentile distance of 237 cells from the blast center, against 37 when every cell stays in the grid (`tests/simulation.test.ts`, "the blast throws debris as far as the prototype did").

The passes run once per superstep, after the offset-1 dispatch (`Flyers.recordSuperstep`):

1. **Launch**: the CA counts, per workgroup, the cells above the launch speed. One workgroup turns the counts into slots after the live flyers (a prefix sum), and each launching cell moves into its slot in the order cell index gives. Past `maxFlyers` the cell stays in the grid, its speed clamped to 1 cell per dispatch, and `capped` counts it.
2. **Flight**: two dispatches of gravity and drag (relative to the air, with the air's pressure gradient pushing, when there is air). A flyer walks the grid cells between its old and new position (4-connected, so it cannot slip between two diagonal cells) and stops in the last free cell before the first occupied one, so a wall one cell thick cannot be crossed. A flyer that stops, or slows below the launch speed, claims the free cell it is in with an atomic minimum on a key made of (cells walked, slot).
3. **Landing, two rounds**: the lowest key of each cell enters the grid with its speed clamped to 1 cell per dispatch and an empty remainder. A flyer that lost looks for a free cell within 3 cells, picked at random so that flyers which wanted the same cell spread out (one buried in the grid searches around the first free cell above it), and tries again in the second round; one that fails stays in the air and counts a retry (`jammed` counts 8 failures).
4. **Compaction**: a prefix sum over the survivors moves them to the other flyer buffer in order.

Every slot comes from a prefix sum in a fixed order and every conflict from the slot index, so two runs with one seed are bit-identical. The brush's erase and replace mark the flyers in the stroke as landed, and the next compaction drops them; Clear empties the list. The display reads an overlay buffer, cleared and filled once a frame with the kind word of each flyer's cell, and draws a flyer where the cell under it is empty.

## Brush

Painting writes the selected element into empty cells within the brush radius of the segment from the previous frame's pointer position to the current one (or into any non-wall cell when Shift is held); erasing empties every cell in range. The pass runs once per frame before the CA steps, so no block ever sees a half-painted state. The radius is in cells, whatever the grid scale. The painted variant comes from the cell coordinates and a per-frame seed, the painted life from the element's `life.start` (0 without one), and the painted temperature from the element's `temperature`; a replaced cell's temperature goes with the rest of it.

### Heat and Cool tools

With the Heat or Cool tool selected, the same brush stroke adds or takes `HEAT_TOOL_RATE` (17 °C, about 1,000 °C a second) per frame from the stored temperature of every cell that holds heat inside it, kept within −273.15 to 9,999 °C (`SimulationInput.heating`, word 9 of the brush uniform). Empty cells, walls and flyers are untouched; the right button still erases. A cell pushed past a threshold changes element on the next dispatch: it holds heat, so its block is never skipped.

## Seeing heat

Three views of the same `temp` words (`src/heat.ts`, `src/shaders/heat.wgsl.ts`, `src/shaders/display.wgsl.ts`):

- **Glow** (normal view): a cell that holds heat and is hotter than 525 °C adds a glow color to its own, dull red at 525 °C, orange at 1,000 °C, yellow-white from 1,600 °C, at a weight that grows to full at 2,000 °C. Adding never darkens a light base color. An element with its own `graphics` function (fire) draws itself and gets no glow.
- **Heat view** (the "Heat view" button, or H; Ctrl, Meta and Alt ignore it): each cell that holds heat is drawn in the color of its temperature (`cellTemp`) from `HEAT_RAMP`, empty space in the background and walls at 30% of their color. Brightness rises with temperature at every step of the ramp, from dark blue at −50 °C through purple (20 °C), red (300 °C), orange (800 °C) and yellow (1,500 °C) to near white at 2,000 °C, so the temperature order never depends on hue alone. A legend above the toolbar labels −50, 100, 500, 1,000 and 2,000 °C.
- **Readout**: the toolbar shows the element and the whole-degree temperature of the cell under the pointer, then the pressure of its air cell (`Sand · 25 °C · pressure +0.20`); empty space shows the temperature of its air; a wall shows its label alone. `Simulation.probe` copies that cell's `kind` and `temp` words and, with air, the pressure and temperature words of its air cell into a staging buffer at the end of the frame's encoder (no extra submit), and `main.ts` reads it about every 100 ms and writes the line with `formatProbe` (`src/readout.ts`). Without air the line ends after the temperature.

A flyer is drawn over an empty cell from its `kind` word alone, so a flying cell has no glow and takes its element color in the heat view until it lands (backlog I17).

## Air

Intuition: empty space is air, and air is not nothing: squeeze it somewhere and the squeeze spreads out as a ring, like a ripple on a pond, and a wall stops the ripple. The air lives on its own coarse grid, much smaller than the cell grid (one air cell covers 4×4 cells by default), because a pressure wave does not need the detail of a grain of sand. It is stepped after the flyers of each superstep. The CA and the flyers read it (see Air moves matter), and the CA puts pressure into it when gunpowder burns or water flashes into steam (see Bursts), and the coupling pass trades heat between it and the cells that hold heat (see Heat and the air). Code: `src/air.ts` (the constants `AIR`), `src/air-grid.ts` (`AirGrid`: buffers, passes, `load`, `clear`, `read`), `src/shaders/air.wgsl.ts` (the passes).

### State

An air cell is `cellSize × cellSize` cells (`airCellSize`: 1, 2, 4 or 8, default 4; `?air=<size>` in the URL, or the `airCellSize` option of `Simulation`), `ceil(W / s) × ceil(H / s)` of them. One `vec4f` per air cell holds the speed of the air through its right face (x) and its top face (y), its pressure at its center (z) and its temperature in °C (w). Putting the speeds on the faces and the pressure in the middle (a staggered grid) keeps the two from drifting apart cell by cell into a checkerboard. Pressure is dimensionless, 0 is ambient. Speeds and `AIR` constants are in cells and dispatches, not air cells, so a different `s` changes the detail of the air and not its speeds. Everything starts, and is reset by `Simulation.load` and `clear`, at rest: pressure 0, speed 0, `ROOM_TEMP`. With `air: false` no buffer is allocated and no pass recorded.

The arrays hold one more column and row than there are air cells: position (a, b) is air cell (a − 1, b − 1), and column 0 and row 0 are ghost cells at pressure 0 and room temperature whose faces are the left and bottom faces of the first real cells, the edge of the world. Beyond the right and top edge the air is read as pressure 0 at room temperature.

### The step

The mask pass runs once per frame, after the brush: for each face it stores the face's openness, and for each air cell the share of powder and liquid cells and the count of cells that hold heat (the velocity pass reads the share; nothing reads the count yet). Then, after the flyer passes of each superstep, `substeps` air steps run, each of five passes in the frame's one compute pass:

1. **Coupling**: moves the pressure the CA released into the burst buffer (a fixed-point `atomic<i32>`, so the sum does not depend on thread order) into the air cell's pressure and clears it (see Bursts), then exchanges heat between the air cell and the cells that hold heat that belong to it (see Heat and the air).
2. **Velocity**: each face gains the pressure difference across it (and, on a top face, the buoyancy of the warm air, see Heat and the air), loses a share (`velocityLoss`) and, for the matter in its air cells, a further share (see Air moves matter), is clamped to `maxSpeed` and multiplied by the face's gate (see Walls). Per air step it gains `waveSpeed² · dt / s` per unit of pressure difference, with `dt = 2 / substeps` dispatches per step.
3. **Pressure**: the pressure falls by the net flow out of the cell times `dt / s`, then loses a share (`pressureLoss`).
4. **Advect**: the speeds are carried along with the air (semi-Lagrangian: a face takes the speed from where its air came from, bilinearly). The temperature crosses faces in flux form, so the heat the air carries is conserved exactly: what leaves a cell enters its neighbor, taken from the upwind side, as the excess over room temperature (so uniform room-temperature air stays put even where it is squeezed). The speed that carries heat is the air's up to `fluxCap = min(maxSpeed, s · substeps / 8)`, the speed at which a cell would hand over all its heat in one step; above it the upwind sum could overshoot.
5. **Diffuse**: the temperature spreads across the open faces (`diffusion`, 2% of the difference per dispatch), and the speeds and pressures are copied back.

Velocity and pressure update in place: the velocity pass writes only speeds and reads only pressures, the pressure pass the reverse. Advect writes the scratch buffer and diffuse copies it back, so every air step ends with the state in the buffer it started in and no buffer parity needs tracking.

The pair of velocity and pressure passes is a wave equation: a pulse spreads as a ring at `waveSpeed` (1 cell per dispatch, measured 1.02 times that at s = 4 and 1.05 at s = 2, the same in the four directions). A wave may cross at most 0.7 air cells per air step and the fastest air at most one, which fixes `substeps = max(1, ceil(2 · waveSpeed / (0.7 · s)), ceil(2 · maxSpeed / s))`: 1 at s = 4 and 8, 2 at 2, 4 at 1.

Example: at s = 4 one air step is one superstep, two dispatches, so the front of a pulse moves 2 cells, half an air cell, per step and reaches the next air cell after 2 steps (`tests/air.test.ts`, AC3 measures the ring's radius against `waveSpeed`).

### Walls and edges

A face is closed by solid cells. The mask pass scans the rows of cells between the centers of the face's two air cells (the columns, for a top face): a row holding a solid cell is closed, and the face's openness is the share of open rows. A wall one cell thick closes every row it crosses at any angle, which a share-of-solid-cells rule would not (a one-cell wall fills a quarter of a 4×4 air cell). A diagonal wall crossing the corner of an air cell still leaves two faces half open, so a face's speed is multiplied by `faceGate = max(0, 2 · openness − 1)` rather than by the openness: a face open on at least half its rows stays a passage, and one open on less is shut, so no path goes through a one-cell wall. In the tests the far side of a one-cell wall, vertical, horizontal or diagonal, stays under 0.03% of the pulse's peak, and a gap of 3 air cells lets 12 to 16% through. Cells outside the grid count as open in the scan, so a wall drawn along the edge seals it.

The world's edge is open air: the faces on it carry no pressure gradient, and the air through them leaves at `waveSpeed` times the pressure inside, which lets a wave out instead of bouncing it back inverted. The outermost ring of air cells also loses `edgeDamping` (10% per dispatch) of its pressure and speed, which takes the echo of waves that arrive at a slant. The echo is under 30% of the wave that arrived at the worst point (a slanted arrival near a corner; 29% at s = 4, 17% at s = 2), and the pressure in the grid has fallen under 5% of a pulse's start within 94 supersteps (`tests/air.test.ts`, AC4). Heat leaves through the edge the same way: the ghost cells stay at room temperature.

### Air moves matter

Intuition: matter sits in the air and is carried and shoved by it. Wind drags a grain toward the wind's speed, and a pressure wave shoves it away from the high pressure, light stuff harder than heavy. Wind does not blow through a pile or a pool, and flames and smoke lean with the wind like anything else that floats.

The CA samples the air once per block, at the block's center, and only for a block that holds a powder, liquid, gas or fire cell (`airAround` in `src/shaders/air.wgsl.ts`, shared with the flight pass): the air's velocity there, bilinear from the faces, and the pressure gradient, in cells per dispatch. The air state is written only by the air passes, between CA dispatches, so this one read outside the block cannot race with the CA (`tests/block-locality.test.ts` binds a random air state and still requires bit-identical results outside the changed blocks). It is binding 1 of the CA's group 3.

- **Drag** (powders and liquids, in `velocityRule` right after gravity): the cell moves its velocity toward the air's by its element's `drag`, `v += drag · (air − v)`, so `drag` is the share of its speed relative to the air that it loses per dispatch. In still air this is the plain `v −= drag · v` a flyer has always had. Example: a grain falling in still air gains `gravity`, then loses `drag` of its speed, each dispatch (`tests/air-matter.test.ts`: its fall stays within 0.05 cells of that recurrence over 23 supersteps). Wind makes a grain lean downwind (the same test file): a thrown grain lands 30 cells downwind of its still-air landing in a steady wind of 0.5 cells per dispatch, and 30 cells upwind in a headwind.
- **Push** (the same step): `v −= push · gradient / density`, with `AIR.push` 16000. Drag alone cannot both let sand fly far (a small drag) and let wind throw it (a large one: advection-only coupling, with a drag strong enough to throw sand, makes sand fall like snow), so the pressure gradient pushes on its own, and lighter matter is pushed harder. Dense sand can move only at its free surface, so a pulse of peak pressure 2 and width 6 cells, 10 cells under the surface of a 40-deep layer, lifts the surface grains out and 86% of the grains that move go away from the pulse's center (`tests/air-matter.test.ts`, measured at 8 supersteps; later the sand around falls back into the pit).
- **Flyers**: the flight pass samples the air at the flyer's position before each of its two dispatches per superstep and applies the same drag and push. Its drag is written `vel · (1 − drag) + drag · air`, which rounds like the flight without air, so in air at rest every flyer word is bit-identical to a run with `air: false`. The flight pass binds the CA's group 3, so it reads the same air buffer.
- **Gases and fire drift** (`driftCell`, a step after the sideways dispersion): a gas or fire cell moves one cell along each axis of the air's velocity, each axis with probability twice the speed along it (certain from half a cell per dispatch), when the cell it moves to is inside the block and empty. It tries the diagonal, then the vertical, then the horizontal move. It keeps no state, so `velocity` and `remainder` stay 0 for anything that does not fall, and in air at rest nothing moves. A cell that has already moved this dispatch (smoke that rose, which is every dispatch in open air) still drifts sideways but not vertically, which would undo its rise. Smoke in air held at a steady sideways speed moves at 0.70 to 0.73 of that speed (`tests/air-matter.test.ts`); gases still rise and spread by their own rules, the air adds a drift on top.
- **Matter slows the air** (the mask pass and the velocity pass): the mask stores the share of powder and liquid cells in each air cell, and a face loses a further `matterLoss` (5% per dispatch) of its speed times the mean of the shares of the two air cells it joins. The air in the middle of a pool or a pile therefore keeps 2% of the wind above it after 30 supersteps.

### Bursts

Intuition: a lit charge of gunpowder, or a drop of water that flashes into steam, pushes the air around it outward; the air then carries the push as a pressure wave (and throws sand, see above).

`Transition.burst` (0 or more, `above` transitions only; a `below` with a burst, a negative or non-finite one is rejected with an error naming the row) is the pressure a cell adds to its air cell when it passes that threshold. The CA releases it in two places: phase 8, when a cell passes any `above` threshold, and phase 6, when a cell ignites by touching fire and its element's `above` becomes fire (gunpowder; oil's burst is 0). The amount is added to the air cell's entry in the burst buffer (binding 2 of group 3) as `round(burst · AIR.burstScale)`, in 1/1024 units, with an integer `atomicAdd`. Integer additions commute, so the sum does not depend on the order the threads add in, and two runs with one seed stay bit-identical. The coupling pass then moves the sum into the pressure and clears the buffer. Without air the burst code is left out of the shader and the buffer does not exist.

Example (`tests/air-burst.test.ts`): gunpowder is 3, water 0.1. On the `explosion` scene (a 48-cell charge buried 12 cells deep in a 48-deep layer of sand on a wall shelf, a fire cell against its left side and a stone wall to its right) the sand within 28 cells of the charge gains a mean distance of 2.54 cells from the center in 8 supersteps, and 84% of the grains that move go outward; unlit, almost none move. At 3 the air is at its speed limit, so more burst only pushes the matter harder. For water, 160 cells poured onto lava raise the pressure around them to a peak of 0.175 and the wind to 0.21 cells per dispatch, while one boiling cell barely stirs the air. A lit charge burns only part of itself; the rest is thrown unburnt (backlog I18).

### Pressure transitions

Intuition: a hard enough blast breaks rock. Stone next to a gunpowder charge is crushed into sand, while stone far away, or behind a wall, feels too little pressure and stays stone.

`ElementDef.pressureAbove` (`{ pressure, becomes }`; `pressure` finite and above 0, `becomes` an element of the table, and the chain of such transitions must not lead back to the row, or the table is rejected with an error naming the row) turns a cell into another element when the air pressure over it passes the limit. It is one way, instant and costs no latent heat. Phase 8 checks it after the temperature thresholds: the CA reads the pressure of the air cell that holds the grid cell (the one a burst from that cell is added to), and when it is above the element's limit the cell is `retype`d to the target. The check exists only when the sim has air and the table has a pressure transition; the air state does not change during a dispatch and nothing is read from the block's neighbors, so the block-locality rule holds. An element with a pressure transition is never inert (`elementInert`), so its block is not skipped.

A crush keeps the cell's heat, not its temperature: the new cell starts at `stored temperature · C_old / C_new`, as at a threshold change, so the heat C·T of a sealed box is unchanged. Stone at 500 °C (capacity 2.2) becomes sand (capacity 1.3) at 846 °C, which can look hotter than the stone it came from for a moment. Stone and sand hold different heat per degree, so keeping the temperature would change the box's total by 7%.

Example (`tests/air-crush.test.ts`): a 48-cell charge on a platform in open air, with single stone cells every 3 cells to its right. `STONE_CRUSH` is 0.2: after 40 supersteps the farthest stone crushed is 13 cells from the charge's edge, the same at 0.18 and 0.22 (10 at 0.25, 25 at 0.14, and at 0.08 every stone out to 61 cells, since the pressure settles to a long low tail below about 0.15). The value sits above the 0.175 peak of water poured onto lava, so boiling water alone does not crush stone. Behind a wall 2 cells thick no stone is crushed, even at a limit of 0.03. A thick stone mass is crushed from its exposed surface only, because an air cell that is half solid or more is closed to the air (backlog I20).

### Cost

On the RTX 4080 at 2560×1340 the air passes at 1x cost 0.07 to 0.12 ms at s = 8, 0.18 to 0.47 ms at s = 4 (default; 0.07 to 0.32 ms of it the coupling pass with its heat exchange, the most on the `thermal` scene), 0.8 to 0.95 ms at s = 2 and about 17 ms at s = 1 (four air steps per superstep over 3.4 million air cells), so 1×1 air is not usable at screen size. The figures at s = 8, 2 and 1 were measured before the heat exchange and are lower bounds. `BENCH_AIR=off,8,4,2,1 npm run bench` measures them.

## Boundaries

`readCell` returns a wall for out-of-bounds coordinates, so the grid edge behaves like a wall, and writes outside the grid are skipped. No rule moves a wall, so out-of-bounds walls never enter the grid. Without fire in the scene every type keeps its count, flyers in the air included (every rule swaps cells); fire burns oil and ends as smoke.
